\chapter*{Abstract}
    In 2004, 70-98\% of websites were found to be inaccessible \cite{lazar_improving_2004}, and one of the user demographics most negatively affected by inaccessible websites is older users \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. Many human-computer interaction researchers have looked at whether different variants of Internet icons create positive performance effects for older users, with generally inconclusive results. 
    
    The goal of this thesis is to measure whether different icon types (specifically text-only, icon-only, or icons with text labels) have a positive impact on older users' performance (as compared to younger users) in a web-based task-completion setting. Specifically, users were assigned to an icon type and then shown six unique websites each containing one task. All research was conducted online and asynchronously. A total of 240 users completed the experiment. After processing data via a mixed-effects linear regression, the text-only condition performed the best, providing not only a boost in accuracy to both age groups, but specifically also a disproportionate decrease in reaction time in the older user population.

\chapter{Introduction} \label{intro}
    In 2004, 70-98\% of websites were found to be inaccessible \cite{lazar_improving_2004}, and one of the user demographics most negatively affected by inaccessible websites is older users \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. One subject that researchers in the field of human-computer interaction (HCI) study in order to create a more accessible Internet for older users is iconography. Even though semiotics has been a constant throughout human history (think written languages), a unique lexicon of icons arose with the onset of the age of technology, defined heavily by Western culture and the newly emerging, globalization-influenced Internet culture \cite{ma_semiotics_1995}, making a brand new barrier to entry for older user populations.

    Many HCI researchers have looked at whether different variants of Internet icons create positive performance effects for older users, but results have generally been inconclusive. For instance, results varied on whether closer semantic distance, meaning “the closeness of meaning between icon and function” \cite{ganor_designing_nodate,liu_study_2023,dosso_how_2021,schroder_effects_2008}, or adding text labels \cite{wu_how_2022,dosso_how_2021,song_developing_2024} positively influences older user’s reaction times and accuracies in ways significantly different than younger users. 

    Most of these studies were also conducted in cognitive psychology-inspired laboratory studies \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}– they trade the web-based context the icons would be embedded into in the real world for isolated, causal inference. There are a couple of north star papers for this thesis that study icon accessibility within a more realistic context. Specifically, Yu and Chattopadhyay presented six feature-rich mobile app user interfaces (UIs; e.g. Starbucks, AMC, Subway, etc.) and studied whether highlighting relevant features for the participants’s given task would boost performance reaction times and accuracies among older adults \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. Excitingly, Yu and Chattopadhyay found that when task-relevant features were emphasized, older adults performed at the same reaction times and accuracies as younger adults during task completion \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. But accessibility iconography studies within task-based environments have been far less explored and do not have the wealth of findings that controlled lab-based iconography studies have.
    
    Causal findings and realism are trade-offs. Yet, given the large strides in technological advancement being pushed, it’s now more important than ever to study accessibility within real-world applications. Alongside this, studying icons in the context of a realistic, task-based experimental design also could provide greater insight into the generally inconclusive findings of laboratory studies. 
    
 	The goal of this experiment is to measure whether different icon types (specifically text-only, icon-only, or icons with text labels) have a positive impact on older users' performance (as compared to younger users) in a web-based, task-completion setting. This experiment embedded these icons into six unique websites each containing one task, such as web browsing and food ordering. All research was conducted online and asynchronously. More specifically, participants were first recruited on Prolific, then redirected to a UX testing platform called Maze where the actual experiment was conducted, which tapped into Figma, a UI/UX design tool, to access these six webpages’ designs. Conducting experiments online is a generally new procedure for many social sciences \cite{jensen_does_2022}, and although HCI pioneers this push towards asynchronous research \cite{lu_understanding_2025}, accessibility research within HCI still primarily conducts studies in-person. With its novelty, a lot of unknowns exist and therefore a lot of scrutiny can be raised, which is addressed later in this paper in Sections \ref{4} and \ref{5}.
    
	A total of 240 users completed the experiment. Results were first examined as descriptive statistics and later plugged into a mixed-effects linear regression model to test for significance. The text-only condition came out significant, providing not only a boost in accuracy to both age groups, but also a disproportionate decrease in reaction time in the older user population, which is the main finding of this paper. Alongside this, the icon with text labels condition overall performs worse than the text-only and icon-only conditions accuracy-wise.

    Although creating positive performance effects for both younger and older users is beneficial and should not be written off as null and void results, the exciting findings are the ones where older users are boosted is ways disproportionately more positively than younger users. This is due to the idea of equity versus equality, where pushing for an equity-based design framework places older users on equal footing to allow them to access and experience technology in the same ways younger users do.

\chapter{Background Literature}\label{2}

In the following chapter, I will discuss the literature motivating my thesis. I will first begin in Section \ref{2.1} by discussing the multidisciplinary origins of human-computer interaction (HCI) and how these origins inform HCI's current-day initiatives. Then Section \ref{2.2} will focus in on the HCI subfield of age-based accessibility and the standards set for accessibility on the Internet. Section \ref{2.3} dives deeper into the academic research that motivates these standards, especially regarding computer iconography, and finally, Section \ref{2.4} discusses task-based research involving older users in HCI and summarizes the gap in the literature that my thesis aims to address.

\section{Human-Computer Interaction} \label{2.1}

    Human-computer interaction (HCI) was born in the 1960s out of the minds of a handful of computer scientists during a time where computers were \enquote{scarce, expensive, bulky and formal-scheduled machines used for automatic calculations} \cite{roussel_looking_nodate}. Their goal was to make computers more compatible with humans to \enquote{\textit{augment the human intellect}} (emphasis in original), according to Douglas Engelbart, one of the founding fathers of HCI \cite{roussel_looking_nodate}. With this clear focus in mind, graphical user interfaces, mouses, applications, and much more were developed to make the computer human-usable \cite{myers_brief_1998}.

    In the 1980s, the tides began to turn as a wave of cognitive psychologists entered the field of HCI. With \enquote{[t]he body of knowledge, research findings and methods that made up cognitive psychology...} \cite{rogers_new_nodate}, HCI gained a robust toolkit and a broader, more holistic worldview.

    With the cognitive psychologists of the 1980s at the helm, the once narrowly-defined goal of HCI quickly expanded, leaving HCI \enquote{bursting at the seams} \cite{rogers_new_nodate}. Fast-forward to current day, HCI research includes everything from how individuals interact with their surrounding technological ecosystem (human-environment interactions), ethics in data privacy, alongside technology's potential in various sectors such as health, social governance, and education \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. 
    
    One of the biggest fields that HCI encompasses is accessibility, which \enquote{aims to ensure that IT applications and services can be used on an equal basis by users with disabilities and older users} \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. The need for expanded accessibility research cannot be overstated, as the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that one in four people in the United States live with a disability \cite{putnam_it_2023}. Accessibility is a subfield that, even within a very diverse parent field, stands out for its multidisciplinary basis. Beyond computer science and psychology, many other fields also inform HCI's accessibility research, including user interface/user experience (UI/UX) design \cite{putnam_it_2023}, ergonomics \cite{myers_brief_1998}, biology \cite{lindberg_how_2006, cruz_user-centered_2023}, economics \cite{nadkarni_task-based_2007}, and more.

\section{Older User Accessibility}\label{2.2}
    As Stephanidis et al.\@ explicitly mentions, one of the key subjects of HCI accessibility research is older users \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. After all, the National Institute of Aging states that people over 60 are one of the fastest growing user groups on the Internet \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005}, and older users' experience of the Internet vastly differs than that of younger users' \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. This is due to age-related declines in vision, which includes things like decreased color discrimination and narrower visual fields \cite{ganor_designing_nodate, cruz_user-centered_2023, lindberg_how_2006, noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1}, memory \cite{ganor_designing_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1}, hearing \cite{noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1, cruz_user-centered_2023}, motility \cite{noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1, cruz_user-centered_2023, putnam_it_2023}, and more. Mahmud et al.~reveal that even the exploration strategies older users employ on technological interfaces are different than other age groups \cite{mahmud_learning_2020}. It should be noted that despite all of the biological developments of aging, older users and society as a whole might not see older individuals as \enquote{disabled}, which uniquely situates them in the wider accessibility discussion. For instance, some perceive this as a a natural phenomenon, whereas others find it hard to box these concurrent declines in ability levels into a singular disability an therefore leave them out of the conversation entirely.

    Yet despite this overwhelming need for accessibility, according to a 2004 paper, 70-98\% of websites are not accessible \cite{lazar_improving_2004}. In order to ease the burden of reaching in the dark to encode accessibility into websites, HCI researchers aim to update web accessibility standards to best match the needs of older users (and generally users with disabilities) today.
    
    The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are hailed as \enquote{the epitome of Web accessibility guidelines} \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005}. The WCAG is targeted towards software developers, allowing them to weave accessibility in even at the granularity of individual lines of code. Recent amendments to the Americans with Disabilities Act also make the WCAG the definitive standard for any institution receiving federal funding, which extends to governmental organizations, higher education establishments, and more \cite{ada_fact_2024}. Other widely-used accessibility standards include Accessible Rich Internet Applications (ARIA), which is derived from UX standards \cite{putnam_it_2023}, and Section 508 of the U.S.'s Rehabilitation Act (29 U.S.C. 795d), which puts forth legal requirements for accessibility within American information and communication technology \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005, noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1, henry2006}. Other countries have also published their own guidelines on web accessibility \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005}. 

\section{Older Users and Icons}\label{2.3}
        But what informs these accessibility standards? Behind the scenes, HCI accessibility researchers have kept pace with the ever-changing realm of technology \cite{chong_bad_2023}, maintaining up-to-date standards even when innovation turns web accessibility on its head. One of these aspects of technology that has undergone substantial development in the mere decades since the inception of the Internet is its icons. 
        
        Iconography is a constant in human history. Semiotics, which is the study of icons, understands them as the foundation of human communication systems, with meanings that are socially and culturally imbued \cite{ma_semiotics_1995}. As the World Wide Web (WWW) emerged, its unique graphical interface naturally brought rise to the WWW's very own lexicon of icons, defined primarily by Western culture and the newly emerging, globalization-influenced Internet culture \cite{myers_brief_1998, ma_semiotics_1995, knight_cultural_2009}. And as icons have emerged as a new mechanism of expressing meaning on the ever-changing Internet, HCI researchers have begun delving into the various facets of this brand new virtual iconography.

    \subsection{Semantic Distance}\label{2.3.1}
        One of these areas of interest is semantic distance, which is defined as \enquote{the closeness of meaning between icon and function} \cite{ganor_designing_nodate}. Results are varied on whether closer semantic distance has a significant positive influence on older users' reaction times and accuracies. 

        Liu et al.~studied the impact of semantic distance (concrete versus abstract) and icon design (skeuomorphic versus flat, which is discussed in Section \ref{2.3.2}) on older versus younger participants' reaction times (RTs) \cite{liu_study_2023}. Specifically, Liu et al.~ended up studying four different icon types-- skeuomorphic concrete, flat concrete, skeuomorphic abstract, and flat abstract. They aimed to measure the resulting cognitive load of participants, which was quantified via performance measures such as reaction time, physiological measures (pupil diameter and the number of fixations), and subjective assessment measures. Participants were given semantic words, then were asked to judge whether the icon shown after the word matched. After analysis via a 3-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA), they found that when icons had further semantic distance, \enquote{older participants had significantly higher reaction times... than younger participants} \cite{liu_study_2023}. 
        
        Dosso and Chevalier also found similar results in their study of semantic distance (close versus far) and text labels (icon versus label versus combination, which will be discussed in Section \ref{2.3.3}) on different age groups (young versus old) \cite{dosso_how_2021}. This study measured RTs (both including rereading and without rereading), correct response scores, and the number and length of rereads when presented with 30 interfaces of icons. Within the interface, there were 20 icons total, 19 being distractors and 1 being the target. Through ANOVAs performed per dependent variable, Dosso and Chevalier concluded that \enquote{older users needed more time to select icons, specifically when semantic distance was far} \cite{dosso_how_2021} as compared to younger users. 
        
        However, the sureness of these findings have been contested, such as by Ganor and Te'eni \cite{ganor_designing_nodate} and Schröder and Ziefle \cite{schroder_effects_2008}. Ganor and Te'eni observed whether differences in an icon's level of detail (simple versus detailed) and semantic distance (close versus distant) created any significant effects on different age groups' (young versus old) performance time and performance accuracy \cite{ganor_designing_nodate}. They achieved this by setting forth 40 tasks, where each task had participants choose the target icon amongst 7 other distractors. Although they did find a greater negative impact of semantic distance on performance on older participants, this impact was not significantly different than younger users according to their ANOVA analyses. 
        
        Schröder and Ziefle also looked at similar independent variables, manipulating icon concreteness and semantic distance (concrete versus abstract) and complexity (simple versus complex) on different age groups' (young versus old) RT and fraction of confirmatory responses per item \cite{schroder_effects_2008}. This resulted in four types of icons, namely abstract simple, abstract complex, concrete simple, and concrete complex icons. Schröder and Ziefle specifically showed mobile phone icons and gave 4 targets and 4 \enquote{dummies} per task for participants to select from. Through a 3-way repeated measures ANOVA, they found that although there was no significant effect of semantic distance on RT, there was a significant effect on fraction of confirmatory responses per item, and still argued that concreteness is a more crucial factor than complexity when designing icons for elderly users despite no significant backing \cite{schroder_effects_2008}. 
        
        Generally, the consensus is that semantically close icons elicit faster reaction speeds, but this has not been confirmed to benefit elderly users more so than the average user in a significant manner.

    \subsection{Skeuomorphic Design}\label{2.3.2}

        As mentioned in Liu et al.\@, the design of icons have also been investigated with somewhat mixed results \cite{liu_study_2023}. Specifically, skeuomorphic icons are of great interest to HCI researchers. Skeuomorphic icons, or skeuomorphisms, are icons that \enquote{are usually designed with 3D effect graphics to imitate real objects through gradients, textures, highlights and shadows, and other visual features to bring users a realistic feeling to help [users] better understand the interface} \cite{liu_study_2023}. These are usually studied in conjunction with flat icons, both of which you can see in Figure \ref{fig:skeuo}. Liu et al.\@, which, as mentioned above, had independent variables of semantic distance and skeuomorphic icon design \cite{liu_study_2023}, and Wu et al.\@, which studied skeuomorphisms in connection to text labels, among other conditions \cite{wu_how_2022}, both found that skeuomorphisms had a positive impact on older users' reaction speeds. 

        \begin{figure}[h!]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{thesistemplate_2020-04-24/figures/skeuo.png}
            \caption{Examples of (a) Skeuomorphic Concrete, (b) Flat Concrete, (c) Flat Abstract, and (d) Skeuomorphic Abstract icons (reprinted by permission of Informa UK Limited, trading as Taylor \& Francis Group, https://www.tandfonline.com)}
            \label{fig:skeuo}
        \end{figure}
        
        Specifically, Wu et al.~looked at icon type (skeuomorphic icon and text, flat icon and text, skeuomorphic icon, and flat icon) and cognitive level on search accuracy, RT, and time to first fixation (the time it takes before glancing at the target icon) \cite{wu_how_2022}. Participants were all 60-66 years old and based in Hangzhou. They were asked to look at 3x3 arrays of icons, with 8 distractors and 1 target, all comprised of icons seen in medical services technology in the regions of Beijing, Shanghai, and Hangzhou. Skeuomorphisms elicited the highest search accuracies, fastest RTs, and fastest times to first fixation (with the order of icon types from best to worst in terms of search performance being skeuomorphic icons and text, flat icons and text, skeuomorphic icon, then flat icon) \cite{wu_how_2022}. These effects seemed to be the results of reduced semantic distance via realistic design. 

        But other studies, such as Xi and Wu, found that flat icons, as opposed to skeuomorphic icons, had higher search performance within older participant groups \cite{xi_influence_2018}. This was found in a study centered around icon style (line, metro, skeuomorphism, and flat) and its effect on total visit duration (per icon) and eye movement contrail (the moving line of sight during a task). Notably, this study's participant group were all graduate students, unlike all other studies within Sections \ref{2.3} and \ref{2.4}. Like Wu et al.\@, icons were arranged in a 3x3 grid, with the same ratio of distractors to target icons \cite{wu_how_2022}. After ANOVA analysis, they found that the total visit duration for flat icons was significantly shorter than skeuomorphic icons. Xi and Wu suggested could be due to the higher cognitive load which comes from deciphering the highly-detailed skeuomorphic designs. 

        Most results point towards skeuomorphic icons over flat icons, however Xi and Wu creates greater nuance in this debate with the introduction of results that show that flat iconography performed better than skeuomorphic icons for a participant pool of graduate students \cite{xi_influence_2018}.

\subsection{Icons and Text Labels}\label{2.3.3}

    A subtopic within accessible iconography that also has inconclusive findings is the impact of text labels. Previously mentioned studies such as Dosso and Chevalier \cite{dosso_how_2021} and Wu et al.~\cite{wu_how_2022}, among others, show that older users perform faster when presented icons with text labels as opposed to icons alone. However, Dosso and Chevalier state that this positive impact was experienced equally by both younger and older age groups \cite{dosso_how_2021}, which is seconded by Song et al.\@'s findings \cite{song_developing_2024}. 
    
    Song et al.\@'s study studied not only text labels, but also icon style (similar, symbolic, example, arbitrary), which mashes together the explorations of semantic distance (Section \ref{2.3.1}) and icon design (Section \ref{2.3.2}) \cite{song_developing_2024}. Participants of different ages (young versus old) were measured in RT and accuracy across 72 tasks, which, like Liu et al.\@, first showed the semantic name, then, like Wu et al.~and Xi and Wu \cite{wu_how_2022, xi_influence_2018}, showed a 3x3 array of 1 target and 8 distractor icons \cite{liu_study_2023}. According to a 2-way ANOVA, the RT of older participants increased across all icon types when text labels were provided, and most exciting of all, older and younger accuracies were equivalent after adding text labels to the icons (which without the text labels were not) \cite{song_developing_2024}. 

    Islam approaches this subtopic of iconography-based accessibility design in a slightly unconventional manner \cite{islam_exploring_2015}. Islam still conducts a laboratory study, but instead of providing analysis just via quantitative measures, Islam centers their behavioral study around semi-structured interviews. Specifically, after sourcing icons from two web domain interfaces, namely a museum webpage and a university webpage, they show the icons to the participants and ask them a series of both open-ended, probing, and closed questions, resulting in interviews between 100 to 120 minutes long. These interviews included questions such as: \enquote{What could be the referential meaning [of this icon]?} and \enquote{How confident are you that you are correct?} \cite{islam_exploring_2015}. Islam's participants were largely graduate students, but not entirely graduate students like Xi and Wu \cite{xi_influence_2018}, with 22 out of 28 being graduate students, 5 out of 28 being full-time company workers, and 1 out of 28 being a research assistant. Through qualitative and quantitative analyses, Islam found that \enquote{the textual interface signs showed better performance in 4 groups; iconic signs showed better performance in 3 groups; and icon[s] with text sign[s] showed better performance in [the] remaining 4 groups} \cite{islam_exploring_2015}, pointing towards no definitive right choice for whether text labels on icons are more accessible across the board for all age groups. 

    So all in all, text labels' impact has been shown to be beneficial for older users, but perhaps not in ways that disproportionately benefit the age group. Specifically, Islam asserts that icons with text labels do not have an universally positive performance impact. However, this icon and text label interaction has not been widely studied, so this research aims to further add to this budding discussion on the impact of text labels on icon accessibility for older users.

\section{Older Users and Task-Based Experimental Design}\label{2.4}
    The vast majority of the studies cited in Section \ref{2.3} are modeled after traditional cognitive psychology methodology, recording reaction times to stimuli presented in isolation without the context of an actual website surrounding said stimuli \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. Isolating the icons does reap strong causal conclusions, but in the vast, virtual universe of the Internet, are icons ever shown in isolation? Causal findings and realism are trade-offs, and given the previously inconclusive laboratory experiments on older users and iconography, studying icons in the context of a realistic, task-based experimental design could provide greater additional insight into this ongoing conversation.

    And research on the negative impacts of website complexity on older user performance reaction times further this point. Nadkarni phrases it best: \enquote{perceived website complexity (PWC) is central to understanding how sophisticated features of a website... affect a visitor's experience} \cite{nadkarni_task-based_2007}. Grahame et al.~defines clutter as a percentage of available page space occupied by text or graphics \cite{grahame_age_2004}. This was primarily studied by controlling for the number of links on a webpage, but Grahame et al.~also manipulated the font size of the links, the placement of the target link on the UI, etc.. The two age groups (young versus old) were shown web pages modeled off of free-to-use templates and were recorded for number of errors, RT, and fixation number. Through ANOVAs calculated per dependent variable, Grahame et al.~determined that increased clutter significantly increased search times and increased error rates for older adults \cite{grahame_age_2004}. 

    And although HCI primarily conducts research via laboratory studies, there is a pocket of HCI that values task-based procedural research. One of the earliest examples of this can be found in 1993, where with the motivation of studying tasks women were \enquote{more likely to perform... in the real world} \cite{czaja_age_1993}, Czaja and Sharit studied how age impacted reaction times for women, ages 25-75 years old, across three different tasks-- namely, data entry, file modification, and inventory management. Data entry was simple and repetitive, requiring the participants to change addresses according to instruction cards. File modification was an intermediary level task, where participants were given a database of high-schoolers organized by grade level and asked to check for consistency between individual student records and instruction cards. Finally, inventory management was the least structured and most demanding task, requiring participants to track the inventory of products from outside suppliers and manipulate a relational database according to instruction cards. Czaja and Sharit showed that increased age, as well as a lack of computer experience, were the greatest determiners of slower response times and a larger number of errors across all three tasks \cite{czaja_age_1993}. 
    
    In Mahmud et al.\@, three age groups (children, adults, and older adults) were studied, each with 10 participants per group \cite{mahmud_learning_2020}. However, this study takes a different approach than Czaja and Sharit-- Mahmud et al.~instead observed the exploratory styles of these three age groups, who all were first-time users of Microsoft's OneNote, when asked to complete four main tasks using the application \cite{mahmud_learning_2020, czaja_age_1993}. These tasks were complex, multistep tasks, including making a new notebook, adding relevant notes to a notebook, and sharing notebooks with others, etc.. After coding video footage of the participants using OneNote and analyzing pre- and post-exploration interviews, Mahmud et al.~found a great treasure trove of findings on older users in embedded, task-based exploration settings. For instance, as many of the other readings mentioned may suggest \cite{liu_study_2023, dosso_how_2021, grahame_age_2004}, older participants were overall slower at task completion as compared to other age groups. Their exploration style tended to not fully explore all options and rely on previous experience with other UIs to their own detriment, and older users ended up experiencing more breakdowns when faced with errors. In fact, older adults typically spent a longer time repeating failed sequences of selections, whereas adults and children more often redirected their efforts based on error messaging. And generally, Mahmud et al.~found that older participants were generally dissatisfied with self-exploration, whereas both children and adults maintained high satisfaction levels and confidence before and after the study. 

    Finally, Yu and Chattopadhyay studied mobile tasks within the context of complex, feature-rich UIs \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. This experiment involved multiple phases, first beginning with a young versus old (each with 30 participants) comparison of task completion given four relevant feature highlighting techniques, where weighted zoom and highlight with context prevailed in terms of task completion time. Yu and Chattopadyay found that with emphasized task-relevant features, older adults performed at the same reaction times and accuracies during task completion as younger adults \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. And it wasn't that the older adults were not initially erroring as much when encountering a difficult task, it was that with the help of search space reduction, these older adults were recovering successfully, as opposed to the behaviors exhibited by older adults when they encountered errors in Mahmud et al.~\cite{mahmud_learning_2020}. The final phase was implementing an AI assistant (Nav Nudge) to reduce the search space, which exhibited 94\% accuracy for their \textit{Query} dataset and 93\% accuracy for their \textit{UILabel} dataset. Yu and Chattopadyay presented the UI of a map application none of the 10 participants (who were all older and weren't a part of the first phase of the study) were familiar with and studied whether providing Nav Nudge, which could decipher speech prompts and highlight the top three most relevant features based on the participant's given task, would boost reaction times and accuracies among older adults (via weighted zoom or highlight with context, as determined by the first part of this study). For example, one of the tasks assigned was: \enquote{Find out how long it will take to travel from Chicago O’Hare Airport to Chicago Union Station using public transit} \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. Specifically, these older adults were first presented with the UI without visual cues, and if they remained stuck on the given task for more than 13 seconds or willingly gave up, they were able to use Nav Nudge to attempt the task once more. As this phase was more exploratory, a qualitative analysis was performed to reveal that Nav Nudge successfully enabled older users in the task completion setting and that older users rated Nav Nudge highly \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. 
    
    If this decomplexification effort can be replicated with icons, whose explicit purpose is to use condensed symbols to represent larger meanings \cite{ma_semiotics_1995}, especially when studied in a task-based experimental design, this would be monumental for older users. As seen in the HCI research above, it is exceedingly rare to find design changes that significantly allow older users and younger users to perform as equals. If substantiated, these findings could introduce a brand new paradigm of Internet iconography and older user UX accessibility.

        
\chapter{Methodology}\label{3}

This chapter discusses the methodology of this study. As an overview, all research was conducted online and asynchronously. This was achieved via a participant matching platform called Prolific for recruitment that then redirected participants to Maze, a UX usability testing platform, to measure reaction times and accuracies in completing six tasks embedded within web interfaces. 

Section \ref{3.1} first justifies the use of Prolific for participant recruitment, especially in light of recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) and its potential harm towards sound online experiment hosting. Then, Section \ref{3.2} runs through the experimental design (Prolific and Maze) and discusses the design choices behind the Figma UI wireframes used. Finally, Section \ref{3.3} discusses the data analysis strategy for both descriptive statistics and linear regression calculations.

\section{In Favor of Online Survey Platforms}\label{3.1}
    Prolific, as the introduction to this chapter states, is an online participant recruitment platform. It allows researchers to post studies to the platform for its exceptionally large user base of over 200,000 active users to participate in \cite{run_prolific}. Surveys can gather large samples in a matter of days or even hours. However, the reliability of the platform has seen significant doubt, especially as AI makes rapid gains in its autonomous decision-making and action-taking abilities.

    I begin this defense by introducing a quote from an analysis of Prolific, which at the time was an up-and-coming participant recruitment platform: 
        \enquote{The success of online experiments is not surprising, as they offer at-scale recruitment of participants in a short time, are generally cheap, and offer access to a broader population – potentially even representative of the internet population – than classical lab experiments} \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}.
        
    And these benefits are especially exciting in the age-based accessibility area of HCI, where recruiting sizable populations of older participants is particularly difficult. For instance, Yu and Chattopadhyay performed a power analysis for the first leg of their study which informed their total of 60 participants \cite{yu_reducing_2024}, and Mahmud et al.\@ had only 30 total participants \cite{mahmud_learning_2020} -- and as a reminder, these participant pools are further divided by age group, with Yu and Chattopadhyay having older and younger age groups and Mahmud et al.\@ having a children, adults, and older adults three-way split. And although the results in these papers are exciting, with such limited sample sizes, it's hard to ensure the generalizability of these findings and the representative diversity of these samples. This is mitigated with Prolific, which has a total \enquote{active, verified, and engaged} participant base of over 200,000 users \cite{run_prolific}.
    
    The cost-effectiveness of Prolific is also a key benefit, but it's of note that Prolific pays an ethical wage, unlike other platforms such as Amazon's Mechanical Turk (MTurk), which is known for \enquote{unreasonably low subject payment} \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}. Specifically, Prolific ensures a minimum fixed payment per unit of time, calculated based on a minimum hourly reward of \$8 U.S. Dollars (USD), with a recommended hourly reward of \$12 USD \cite{how_prolific}. 

% might not actually wanna add... still kind of low lol
%And even though the sample size for this study goes significantly beyond what has previously been done in this field (200 total, 100 per age group), compensating each individual fairly does not break the bank-- even with the \$12 USD recommended rate, given the short estimated length of this experiment (5 minutes), each person gets \$1 USD for participating, which with Prolific fees ended up costing \$266.40 USD.

    Online survey platforms such as Prolific are no silver bullet, though. There's longstanding belief that these participants, in order to make cash out quick, speed through experiments to earn their incentive and move on to the next survey \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}. This fear is only amplified with the onset of AI in an online survey context. Peterson and Asher et al.\@ both studied AI-generated free responses on MTurk and Prolific respectively \cite{peterson_impact_nodate, asher_chatbots_2025}. Peterson found that just 20\% of the 1443 responses passed quality checks and counted as usable data \cite{peterson_impact_nodate}, whereas Asher et al.'s findings on Prolific was a little more hopeful-- when using a keystroke tool to flag submissions with an unusually low number of keystrokes, they found that only 9\% were AI-assisted responses \cite{asher_chatbots_2025}. But generally, MTurk is more vulnerable to AI responses \cite{christoforou_generative_2024}, partially due to its unethical wages, which encourages long-tail exploitation that oftentimes isn't valuable enough to humans to engage in \cite{carlini_llms_2025}. And now, with the rise of commercially-available AI models that can easily execute computer tasks, such as the open-source OpenClaw \cite{openclaw}, OpenAI's Operator \cite{openai_operator}, Claude's Sonnet 3.0 \cite{claude_sonnet}, and Google's Gemini 2.5 \cite{google_gemini}, things look even more bleak for online survey models such as Prolific. Given their novelty, little research has been done on the topic of so-called \enquote{computer use} AI models participating in online surveys yet. It's worth noting that, however, these autonomous agents run locally, meaning there is a slightly higher barrier to entry as compared to web-hosted AI chatbots.

    Luckily, Prolific takes measures against AI and participants who rush through studies. Prolific allows researchers to manually approve study participants for payment, which can help researchers screen for participants with unusually fast completion times or other suspicious activity \cite{prolific_home}. During the writing of this paper, Prolific is beta-ing an option to automatically \enquote{[r]eject exceptionally fast submissions} and to detect AI-generated written responses, which also deters against these bad actors \cite{prolific_home}. But none of these absolutely destroy the threat. In contemplating trade-offs, both via reading the literature presented above and discussing with Reed faculty and staff (such as Psychology Professor Greg Jensen, who has published research employing Prolific for recruitment \cite{jensen_does_2022}), the ability to seamlessly recruit a notably difficult demographic at such large numbers outweighed the threat of falsified data. If anything, by bulking up the sample size, it mitigates the effect of a handful of AI-submitted-responses, if any. But this is not to say that the risk is zero, which is a hard truth kept front-of-mind throughout the following phases of this thesis. 

    Another concern lies in whether the results will be overall faster than average because some online survey users tend to rush through surveys to earn more, and that generally these users will be more fluent in computer technology given that they actively participate on online survey platforms such as Prolific. This concern is valid, however, this is a bias that could have also occurred in an in-person study as well, although perhaps not to the same extent. To gather a significant number of older users, one could suggest recruiting them in community spaces elderly folks frequent, such as the library and senior living communities, but people who would willingly participant may be more confident and skilled in computer use than the average older user. And in terms of diversity and sheer quantity, online recruitment wins over local recruitment. 

    All in all, Prolific is not the perfect recruitment tool. But in order to get significant results and to ensure smooth recruitment and data collection, this study has decided to employ Prolific to meet its ends.

\subsection{Maze and User Tests}\label{3.2.2}
    As mentioned above, Maze is a UX testing platform. This means that UI/UX professionals use this platform to test different design choices for their interfaces and record relevant data from users. There are various platforms of this nature, including PlaybookUX, Userbrain, Lookback, etc., but Maze was chosen for pricing and recorded data types (including click heatmaps), amongst other features \cite{unmoderated_user-testing_tools-nng_nodate}. Maze was also used in a previous Reed Psychology thesis on UX design \cite{bec_psychology_2023}, which also informed this choice.

    In Maze, participants are once again screened for the attributes listed above in Section \ref{3.2.1}. They are additionally asked to input their Prolific IDs, which allowed for compensation post-experiment. Then, after implying their consent, the meat of the study began, starting with general study instructions. The instructions read as follows: \\

    \noindent\fbox{%
        \parbox{\textwidth}{You will be presented with 6 different websites. For each website, you will be given a task. Please click the correct feature to complete the task. In the case that tasks are complex and require 1+ steps, please click on the correct feature for the first step of the task. You are encouraged to complete these tasks as accurately and quickly as possible.\\

        Click continue to proceed.
        }%
    }
    \bigbreak

    Then, participants were randomly placed into one of three groups. Each group was exposed to a different variant of icon type (text-only, icon-only, icon with text labels); an example of each of these variants is shown below in Figure \ref{3vars}. All groups were exposed to the same six web UIs and tasks in the same order. Specifically, for each of the six tasks, the participants would first see a screen with the task on it, then would have to click to proceed to the web UI to complete the task. As explained in the instructional blurb above, completion of a task would be measured by when the participant clicked on the correct icon to complete the first step of a task. Creating UIs participants could interact with was achieved with a Figma prototype, which will be explained more in Section \ref{3.2.3} below.

    \subsection{Figma and UI/UX Design Choices}\label{3.2.3}
        The six website types were chosen to mimic popular website functions, which was quantified through Cloudflare's Top 100 Domains list \cite{cloudflare}. The six chosen website types were: search engine (e.g. Google Search), e-commerce (e.g. Amazon), food ordering (e.g. GrubHub), hotel booking (e.g. Expedia), library (e.g. OverDrive), and video streaming (e.g. YouTube). In order to make realistic web UIs, the wireframes were modeled after mainly real Australian (thus, not frequented by Americans) webpages. The only two non-Aussie sites were Mojeek and Gom Player, which were selected nonetheless for their relative obscurity. Australian websites were chosen over non-English webpages to avoid translation errors (and Australian websites were chosen over other English-speaking foreign countries because of the author's familiarity with various Aussie domains). The chosen model webpages were \textit{Mojeek} for search engine \cite{mojeek}, \textit{Universal Store} for e-commerce \cite{universal_store}, \textit{Fishbowl} for food ordering \cite{fishbowl}, \textit{Vulcan Hotel} for hotel booking \cite{vulcan}, \textit{Penrith City Libraries} for library \cite{penrith_libraries}, and \textit{Gom Player} for video streaming \cite{gom_video_player}. The different model websites were also chosen to have a variety of perceived complexity, which was qualitatively assessed upon selection. Simply put, complexity was determined by the number of buttons on screen (which is akin to Grahame et al.\@'s definition of clutter \cite{grahame_age_2004}), the placement of said buttons, and the difficulty of the task paired with the website UI.

        All mockups were created on Figma, an industry-standard design and prototyping tool used specifically for diagramming technological user interfaces (UIs). It has a wide range of capabilities, including prototyping, that allow UI/UX designers to not only mock up their UIs, but also add interactive elements to these otherwise stagnant UIs \cite{what_is_figma}. You can find all UI/UX designs/prototypes made for this thesis at the following link: \url{https://www.figma.com/design/AYTVQfj9zkuQJA3DTUvs96/THES-2.0?node-id=0-1&t=NpvtR9ArWRiOFMZa-1}.

To use the terminology introduced in Sections \ref{2.3.1} and \ref{2.3.3}, the \textit{Material Design Icon Library} consists of flat icons of varying semantic distances (with some abstract and some concrete). The \textit{Material Design Icon Library} has integrations with Figma as well, allowing the icons to be altered in terms of dimension and color to match the aesthetics of these UIs better. This library was also chosen for its previous use in Song et al. \cite{song_developing_2024}.  

        Most importantly, Figma has prototypes, which allow UI/UX designers to introduce interactive components to their wireframes. In order to create three separate groups per variant (text-only, icon-only, icon with text labels), there were three prototyped user flows or sequences in which users viewed the Figma mockups. The ordering of the websites was consistent between groups, the only thing that changed was the icon type on each site. As mentioned in Section \ref{3.2.2}, users from all groups first saw the instructions for tasks. Descriptions of tasks were centered on an otherwise blank Figma frame; when clicked, they would redirect the user to the corresponding web UI where the task would take place. Each participant group saw a different variant of the website. This was achieved by creating three of each task frame that would redirect to the correct variant upon user interaction, which you can see in Figures \ref{figma_wideshot} and \ref{figma_zoom}. All web UIs were coded such that the users were redirected to the next task frame when the correct feature corresponding to the task was clicked. Upon completing the sixth and last task, users were redirected to a blank frame. This blank frame was what Maze recognized as the end of the user flow, which would conclude the Figma prototype and allow users to continue through Maze. The Figma user flows were directly embedded into Maze via plug-in.

\section{Data Analysis}\label{3.3}
    \subsection{Data Recording and Migration}\label{3.3.1}
        All data was recorded by Maze and migrated by hand into an Excel spreadsheet. The spreadsheet was formatted such that each participant had six entries, one per website. Each entry contained the following information: 
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item $MazeID$ (a string of integers)
            \item $ProlificID$ (a string of integers and characters)
            \item $Age$ (either $18-35$ or $60+$)
            \item $Icon$ (either $TextOnly$ [$TO$], $IconOnly$ [$IO$], or $IconWithTextLabels$ [$IWTL$])
            \item $Website$ (an integer between 1-6 corresponding to the order of appearance of the website within the user flow)
            \item $ReactionTime$ (floating point number, in seconds)
            \item $MisclickRate$ (floating point number, represented as a percentage of wrong clicks over total number of clicks)
        \end{enumerate}

        A couple of notes: First, $Age$ will be referred to interchangeably as age group and $Icon$ will be referred to interchangeably as icon type from here on out. Alongside this, $Participant$ and $MazeID$ will be used interchangeably in Sections \ref{3.3.4} and \ref{4.2}. $MazeID$ was chosen over $ProlificID$; it also reflects the code (which can be found at the Github repository linked below).
        
        User responses were only migrated to Excel if that user had fully completed the study, meaning that they found correct solutions to all six websites. Users who did not complete all six websites or dropped out before being assigned to an \textit{Icon} condition (which is an important distinction for later in Section \ref{3.3.3}) were not included within the final Excel sheet.
        
        The spreadsheet ended up containing a total of 1440 rows, corresponding to 240 unique participants. In order to process the data in Python (see Section \ref{3.3.2}), the Excel spreadsheet was first converted into a \textit{.csv} file.

    \subsection{Tech Stack}\label{3.3.2}

        All data analysis was performed in Python, which was enabled by various data-related Python libraries. The code for all of this study's data analysis, as well as the anonymized \textit{.csv} file mentioned in Section \ref{3.3.1}, can be found in the Github repository linked here: \url{https://github.com/00eemsy/thesis}.
    
        The primary Python library employed was \textit{pandas} (lowercase intentional), a popular, open-source data analysis library \cite{pandas}. This was used to access the data within the \textit{.csv} file, clean the data, and to perform data manipulation required for both the descriptive statistics and actual linear regression (see below in Sections \ref{3.3.3} and \ref{3.3.4}). For instance, \textit{pandas} was employed in the descriptive statistics to calculate the average $ReactionTime$ of participants per $Website$ (see Table \ref{avg_speed}).
    
        \textit{Matplotlib}, another popular, open-source Python library, was used for data visualization \cite{matplotlib}. All of the data visualizations found in this thesis, from bar charts, histograms, to scatterplots, were constructed by \textit{Matplotlib} with \textit{pandas} findings. 
    
        Besides these main two libraries, several other libraries were employed in one-off cases, including \textit{statsmodel} (lowercase intentional) for mixed-effects linear regression calculations \cite{statsmodel_mixedlm}, \textit{scipy} (lowercase intentional) for chi-squared calculations \cite{scipy_chisquare}, and \textit{numpy} (lowercase intentional) for array manipulation in tandem with \textit{pandas} calculations \cite{numpy}.

    \subsection{Descriptive Statistics} \label{3.3.3}
        Performing descriptive statistics analysis before the formal linear regression was necessary to achieve multiple goals. The main goal of these descriptive statistics, besides creating some casual familiarity with the data, was determining exclusion criteria. As mentioned above in Section \ref{3.1}, one of the biggest drawbacks to online survey platforms is noisy data. This can be caused by participants speeding through instructions \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}, randomly clicking through UX tests, or even AI bot inaccuracies \cite{peterson_impact_nodate, asher_chatbots_2025, christoforou_generative_2024} (see more in Section \ref{3.1}). So cleaning up data was a priority to isolate the signal within the variance.

        The second goal of these descriptive statistics was making sense of the drop-off rate ($DropOff$) for those assigned an $Icon$ variant. As mentioned in Section \ref{3.3.1}, this is related, but distinct, from those who dropped-off before being assigned a variant and total drop-off rate. Specifically, their relationship is as follows:

        $$DropOff_{\text{Total}} = DropOff_{\text{Assigned Icon Type}} + DropOff_{\text{Not Assigned Icon Type}} $$
        
        Maze recorded a total of 393 unique attempts (meaning there was an initial participant pool of size 393), but only 240 successfully completed attempts, was a cause for concern given the $\sim38.9\%$ $DropOff_{\text{Total}}$. To this end, descriptive statistics were supposed to shed light on what created this large $DropOff_{\text{Total}}$, and whether this had something to do either with the areas of interest (age group and icon type), which is why we look specifically at $DropOff_{\text{Assigned Icon Type}}$.

        In terms of exclusion criteria, most of the work was looking at the dependent variables (DV; $ReactionTime$, $MisclickRate$) in relation to the various independent variables (IV; $Age$, $Website$, $Icon$, etc.). Average \textit{ReactionTime} was considered both per \textit{Website} and summed across all six $Website$s. To help contextualize these calculations, standard deviation was also calculated. More detailed numbers will be discussed in Section \ref{4} below. 

        This data was then plotted in various manners to help visualize the information.         As for $DropOff_{\text{Assigned Icon Type}}$ calculations, the main route for analysis was a chi-square test of independence. A chi-square test of independence decides if two categorical variables are associated \cite{discovery_chi-square}. The first chi-square test was performed between the number of participants per $Age$ and $Icon$ who finished the experiment versus those who did not finish but were assigned a variant. The second chi-square test was performed twice, once per $Age$ variant, between the number of participants per $Icon$ who finished the experiment versus those who did not finish but were assigned a variant. To clarify, those who dropped off before making it to the websites were not included in this measure. 

    \subsection{Mixed Effects Linear Regression} \label{3.3.4}

    Many hypothesis testing methods were considered before settling on a mixed effects linear regression. First, a t-test, a simpler model of hypothesis testing, was considered. These are ideal for testing between two conditions, but given that there is a 5\% false positive error rate per test that compounds \cite{one-way}, comparing between more than two variants (given the three \textit{Icon} variants) was not viable. This could be mitigated by combining scores between variants (for instance, comparing $IWTL - IO$ versus $TO - IO$), but this was not as straightforward and therefore not pursued.

    ANOVAs were also considered, mainly because of their frequent appearance in the literature \cite{ganor_designing_nodate, schroder_effects_2008, liu_study_2023, dosso_how_2021, wu_how_2022, xi_influence_2018, song_developing_2024, grahame_age_2004, yu_reducing_2024}. And this seemed logical, as an ANOVA is mathematically identical to a linear regression as a special type of linear model. But this is confounded by the introduction of the \textit{Participant} random effect. Since each participant brings baseline variations in reaction time and accuracy, and each participant completes six tasks across the six websites, observations were not independent of one another due to random variation between participants. Classical ANOVAs assume independence between observations \cite{one_way_anova}, and repeated-measures ANOVAs, despite allowing for non-independence between observations, has less flexibility than necessary to process this data (for instance, it cannot handle unbalanced data well, which Table \ref{finish} shows is necessary) \cite{muhammad_guidelines_nodate}. On the other hand, mixed effects linear regressions are naturally able to handle this co-dependence with a random effect, which below is represented as $u_i$.

    Finally, the exact formulation of the linear regression has changed over time. Originally, the formula was:
    $$Outcome\sim Age + Icon + Website +(Age * Icon)$$ 
    
    in R notation and 
    
    \begin{gather*}
        DV = \beta_0 + \beta_1(Age_{60+})+(\beta_2(Icon_{IWTL})+\beta_3(Icon_{TO}))+\\(\beta_4(Website_2)+\beta_5(Website_3)+\beta_6(Website_4) + \beta_7(Website_5) + \beta_8(Website_6)) + \epsilon
    \end{gather*}

    when written out in full.
    
    This equation measures DVs (either \textit{ReactionTime} or \textit{MisclickRate}) from the main effects of \textit{Age}, \textit{Icon}, \textit{Website}, and the interaction term between age group and icon type ($(Age*Icon)$). However, this misses a key random effect, namely that of $Participant$, since baseline variance between users cannot be controlled for.

    The final equation required a mix of both fixed and random effect variables, hence why this thesis employs a mixed effects linear regression for the bulk of its analysis. In R-speak, this equation is 
    
    $$Outcome\sim Age + Icon + Website +(Age * Icon) + (1|Participant)$$
    
    This is equivalent to

    \begin{gather*}
        DV_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(Age_{60+})_i+(\beta_2(Icon_{IWTL})+\beta_3(Icon_{TO}))_i+\\(\beta_4(Website_2)+\beta_5(Website_3)+\beta_6(Website_4) + \beta_7(Website_5) + \beta_8(Website_6))_j + u_i + \epsilon_{ij}
    \end{gather*}

    This is later amended to and finalized as the following after applying exclusion criteria (which is explained more in Section \ref{3.3.4}):\\
    \begin{center}
        \fbox{\parbox{12.5cm}{$DV_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(Age_{60+})_i+(\beta_2(Icon_{IWTL})+\beta_3(Icon_{TO}))_i+\\(\beta_4(Website_4) + \beta_5(Website_5) + \beta_6(Website_6))_j + u_i + \epsilon_{ij}$}}\\
    \end{center}
    \bigbreak
    
    As mentioned previously, $u_i$ represents the $Participant$ random effect. The $i$ indexes individual participants, whereas $j$ indexes $Website$s, meaning $i\in[1,240]$ and $j\in [1,6]$. The indices correlate to the two levels of observation, namely the $Website$-level observations ($j$) (this includes all $Website$ terms) and the $Participant$-level ($i$) (this includes $Age$, $Icon$, and $u_i$).

    With so many terms, overfitting was a risk. Overfitting occurs when terms are added to a model to the point that the model no longer represents the generalizable phenomenon and instead models the specific dataset instead \cite{perraillon}. In order to avoid this, an additive approach was employed, meaning that terms were added onto the model one-by-one and model iterations were compared at each step for relative fitness. Model 0 was the intercept-only model, model 1 added in \textit{Age}, model 2 added in \textit{Icon}, model 3 added in \textit{Website}, and finally, model 4 added in the ($Age*Icon$) interaction term. Furthermore, Akaike Information Criterion, the comparative performance metric for regression models employed, also further negates this risk. AIC for linear regressions is:

    $$AIC_p = n\times \ln\left(\frac{SSE_p}{n}\right)+2p$$

    The parameters of AIC are as follows: $p$ is the number of estimated parameters, $n$ is the number of observations, and $SSE$ is the residual sum of squares. Essentially, this statistic estimates the amount of information lost when using a certain model \cite{aic}. One AIC's magnitude on its own does not relay any information about goodness of fit, it is when it's employed comparatively to other models' AICs where $\Delta AIC = AIC_{i-1}-AIC_{i}$ matters \cite{burnham_information_2002}. That being said, the lowest AIC retained the most information, meaning it was the more accurate out of the compared models, meaning the term added provided substance to the model's predictive power without overfitting the data.

    There are several metrics for comparing performance between regression models, including $R^2$, but $AIC$ was the best choice because it penalizes overfitting and balances goodness of fit and parsimony well \cite{perraillon}. Specifically, the $+2p$ penalizes having a larger number of parameters \cite{perraillon}, which makes it better for combating overfitting as compared to other statistics such as $R^2$.
    
    When calculating AIC, several pairs of models were tested. The difference in AIC between models 0 and 1 was looked at to assess the impact of \textit{Age} on the model. Models 1 and 2 were compared to estimate $Icon$'s influence, models 2 and 3 estimated $Website$'s influence, and finally, the $\Delta AIC$ of models 3 and 4 assess the importance of $(Age*Icon)$. Most important for our study was seeing how the interaction between $Age$ and \textit{Icon} affected the model's fitness. 

    After deriving the most fit model, the significance levels of the different variants were derived in comparison to \enquote{baseline} conditions (for instance, $IO$ was chosen as the baseline for \textit{Icon}, meaning $IWTL$ and $TO$ were compared against $IO$'s results). 
 
    This mixed effects linear model approach was employed on a plethora of exclusion criteria, which were determined via descriptive statistics, which are discussed in Section \ref{3.3.3} and \ref{4.1}. Tests were done with the following exclusions:
    
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item None
        \item $Websites$ 1 and 3
        \item Average $MisclickRate$ $>80\%$
        \item Total $ReactionTime$ $>200$s
        \item Total $ReactionTime$ $>200$s or Average $MisclickRate$ $>80\%$
    \end{enumerate}

    The second exclusion criteria, excluding all results from $Website$s 1 and 3 entirely, was tested because $Website$s 1 and 3 consistently performed worse in terms of both DVs (which will be discussed more thoroughly in Section \ref{4}). To conjecture, $Website$ 3 was a complex, visually busy UI (see Figure \ref{w3}) with a difficult task that relied on clicking a small button (\enquote{Remove Mixed Greens from Spicy Salmon order}), making for worse results across both DVs. And $Website$ 1, despite having a relatively simpler UI due to being a search engine (see Figure \ref{3vars}), had comparable levels of error possibly due to its appearance as first in the sequence of $Website$s for all users. This user behavior was discovered during an informal, one-man pilot study completed by advisor Dr.\@ Adam Groce, where he clicked too fast on the first screen and skipped past vital instructions for completion of $Website$ 1's task. If PhD doctors are not immune to this, it is probably a reasonable conjecture to generalize this phenomenon to the larger pool of participants.

\section{Mixed Effects Linear Regression}\label{4.2}
    The exclusion criteria settled upon was excluding results from $Website$s 1 and 3 entirely, as justified in Section \ref{4.1.1}. The following linear regression results were calculated using the dataset with exclusion criteria applied. 

    As aforementioned in Section \ref{3.3.4}, linear regression models were computed in an additive manner, meaning that variables were inserted into the equation one by one to see whether they made a marginal impact on the fitness of the linear regression. Table \ref{models} formally defines what each model version measures in R-speak. Note that each model is grouped by $Participant$, hence the $Participant$ random effect (written as $(1 | MazeID)$).
    
    \begin{table}[H]
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{| p{1.5cm} | p{13cm} |}
            \hline
            Model & Definition\\
            \hline
            Model 0 & $DV\sim1 + (1 | MazeID)$\\
            \hline
            Model 1 & $DV\sim Age + (1 | MazeID)$\\
            \hline
            Model 2 & $DV\sim Age + Icon+(1 | Maze)$\\
            \hline
            Model 3 & $DV\sim Age + Icon + Website+(1 | MazeID)$\\

    DV is equivalent to either $ReactionTime$ or $MisclickRate$, depending on what's measured. This impact was measured with an Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to determine the model of best fit. The model with the lowest AIC comparatively fits the best.

    To break it down even further, the $\Delta AIC$ between two models with only one term different between them implies the difference in support of this model over another, and therefore how much of the variance in data is explained by adding this term to the preexisting model \cite{burnham_information_2002}. Namely, for $\Delta AIC=(\text{Model with less terms}) - (\text{Model with more terms})$, $0<\Delta AIC < 2$ means the models have equivalent levels of support, $4<\Delta AIC <7$ means the model with the added term has some support for being a better fitted model, and $\Delta AIC >10$ means that the model with the added term has substantially better support for being a model of better fit. This does not mean that in general these variables hold significance in respect to the DV, it just means that the variable identifies some signal amidst variance between the two models being compared.

\section{Robustness Checks}\label{4.3}

    In the following section, the results of the linear regression discussed above in Section \ref{4.2} are compared to regressions performed on similar, alternative exclusion criteria. 

    After settling upon the exclusion criteria of no $Website$s 1 and 3, the alternative criteria to confirm robustness were:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item None
        \item (No $Website$s 1 and 3) or ($>200$s total $ReactionTime$ or $>80$\% $MisclickRate$)
        \item (No $Website$s 1 and 3) or ($>200$s total $ReactionTime$)
        \item (No $Website$s 1 and 3) or ($>80$\% $MisclickRate$)
    \end{enumerate}

    Criteria $2-4$ were all chosen because of the alternative exclusion criteria listed in Section \ref{3.3.4}. As a reminder, in the case of criterion 2, if either the user's total $ReactionTime$ was above 200 seconds or their average $MisclickRate$ was over 80\%, all of their results would be excluded in line with Figure \ref{tsvamr}. When split into criteria 3 and 4, this logic still holds-- if a user doesn't meet the threshold, all of their results are removed from analysis.
    
    Given the Figure \ref{tsvamr}, the original exclusion criteria 2-5 had some justification, although not as strong of a justification as the chosen criteria. Therefore, there is still merit in studying the union of both exclusion criteria, since it's worth looking at whether the findings from Section \ref{4.2} hold under either defensible exclusion strategy.

    The other options for exclusion criteria mentioned in Section \ref{3.3.4} were also tried independently, but deemed not as relevant to this thesis. Other robustness checks were also performed, but also deemed not as relevant. Both of these findings, alongside more complete tables for the data shown below, can still be found in the Appendix (see Appendix \ref{c}).

Then, Section \ref{5.2} will bring to light, confront, and acknowledge the various limitations of this thesis. Finally, future directions will be suggested in Sections \ref{5.3} and \ref{5.4}.

\section{Interpretations}\label{5.1}
    Two main results follow from Chapter \ref{4}, namely that the $TextOnly$ condition has a disproportionately positive influence on the 60+ age group (and that $TO$ overall performs better across age groups than $IO$ and $IWTL$), and that the $IconWith Text Labels$ condition overall performs worse than the $TO$ and $IO$ conditions. A reminder that these results hold for $Websites$ 2, 4, 5, and 6.

\subsection{Text-Only and 60+ Interpretations}\label{5.1.1}
    To reiterate, when modeling $ReactionTime$, the best model of fit included the $Age$ and $Icon$ interaction terms (see Section \ref{4.2.1}). More specifically, the interaction term between the 60+ age group and the $TO$ icon type had a coefficient of -3.018 and a p-value of 0.018. In other words, the 60+ age group averaged 3.018 second less on tasks that were shown in the $TO$ condition than the 18-35 group.

    As for $Misclick Rate$ and $TO$, in both semi-complete model 3 and fully-expanded model 4, $TO$ by itself had significance (p-values=0.001, 0.037), both with negative coefficients (coefficients=-8.351, -7.813). However, the interaction term from above was not significant in $Misclick Rate$.

    These findings, especially the significance of the $TO$ and 60+ interaction in relation to $ReactionTime$, are incredibly exciting. As explained in Chapter \ref{2}, one of the main focuses of the age-based accessibility subfield of HCI is finding ways to even the technological playing field for older users experiencing age-related physical and mental declines. Of course, finding accessibility fixes that benefit all ages is also exciting, and in practice can be employed in building accessibility technology. But the grand prize is discovering tweaks that overall benefit both age groups but ultimately benefit older users more than younger users. It's in line with notions of equality versus equity-- one makes sure all are given equal resources despite different starting lines, and the other ensures all are given adequate resources to make the playing field completely equal. 

    This result finds backing in scholarship, as well. As mentioned in Section \ref{2.3.1}, the closer the semantic distance icons are to what they represent, the faster participants are. And what closes the semantic distance more than language itself? However, there is debate as to whether semantic distance provides a larger performance boost to older users versus younger (see Section \ref{2.3.1}). To recap, Liu et al.\@ found that further semantic distance disproportionately hindered the $RT$s of older participants, as compared to younger participants \cite{liu_study_2023}; Dosso and Chevalier also found similar results \cite{dosso_how_2021}. But these age-based discrepancies are contested by Ganor and Te'eni \cite{ganor_designing_nodate} and Schröder and Ziefle \cite{schroder_effects_2008}, so this thesis' findings add a new perspective into this inconclusive HCI debate.

    But as seen in Chapter \ref{2}, of the literature written on icon accessibility, very few include a $TextOnly$ condition. To some extent, the reason is obvious-- in studying iconography, the goal is to condense large amounts of word-based meaning into a visual iota, usually for UI/UX design purposes. But this suggests exploration into a middle ground between alienating language entirely and alienating design principles completely might hold merit. 

    Of course, this study's results do not come without room for questioning. One of the main conjectures that will be presented to contextualize the negative impact of $IWTL$ below is that the UI had high levels of clutter, to use Grahame et al.\@'s terminology \cite{grahame_age_2004}. Although the positive $ReactionTime$ effects of $TO$ are safely significant (p-value=0.018), it could be possible that they benefited from lack of clutter, although this would not account for the significant difference between $TO$ and $IO$ specifically. And another possibility is that \enquote{digital native} 18-35 year olds might not have been used to associating text with buttons, especially without the explicit hyperlink formatting (which the $TO$ condition was not presented in). Without this prior conditioning, it could have been the case that $TO$ would no longer significantly boost older users over younger users. But with significant results in $Misclick Rate$ that extends equally to both groups, this is unlikely. 

\subsection{Icon with Text Labels Interpretations}\label{5.1.2}
    The significant results related to the $Icon With Text Labels$ condition were found within models run with $Misclick Rate$ as the dependent variable. In the model of best fit, model 3, $IWTL$ had a coefficient of 6.225 and a p-value of 0.019. Thus, $IWTL$ made $Misclick Rate$ higher, specifically by 6.225\%, across the board for both groups within $Age$, as compared to $IO$ and $TO$. For transparency, these results were not significant in model 4, for either $IWTL$ by itself (p-value=0.330) or the 60+ and $IWTL$ interaction term (p-value=0.331); $IWTL$ was also not significantly worse in $ReactionTime$ for any model.

    These results directly conflict with the consensus within the literature. Generally, $IWTL$ conditions reap faster and more accurate results as compared to $IO$ conditions. To summarize, Dosso and Chevalier observed that labeled icons gave older participants reaction time boosts on par with their younger counterparts \cite{dosso_how_2021}. This finding is seconded by Wu et al.\@ \cite{wu_how_2022}. Wu et al.\@ specifically found that skeuomorphic icons with text outperformed its flat icons with text and unlabeled counterparts. Song et al.\@'s work argues that icons presented with text labels enabled older users to perform at the same accuracy levels as their younger counterparts \cite{song_developing_2024}. There is some contention from Islam, whose combined quantitative and behavioral study concluded there was no frontrunner between the icon-only and icon with text labels conditions \cite{islam_exploring_2015}, but the sect within HCI that disagrees with an added text label's positive performance benefits is small.

    So why does $IWTL$ significantly and negatively impact accuracy in this experiment? To propose an explanation, these studies were all conducted in a laboratory setting where icons (or icons with text labels) were shown in isolation. This is not the case in an embedded, task-based setting such as this thesis. Icons now exist within a fuller context, which brings along confounding variables such as clutter \cite{grahame_age_2004} that do not exist within a controlled, sterile environment. This is both a combination of a change in environment as well as experimental design error, which especially shone through when combined with already complex and information-dense UIs such as $Website$ 3 (see Figure \ref{w3}).

\section{Limitations}\label{5.2}
    Now, we move towards the limitations of this study, some of which were touched upon by Section \ref{5.1} already.

    The first order of business is acknowledging faulty experimental design. This existed in terms of UI/UX design choices (see $Website$ 1 and 3's results in Section \ref{4.1.1}), specifically in terms of the general instructions provided to participants. Although much of the error that arose with $Website$ 1 could be attributed to user error, some of it could have originated in unclear instructions. An example of an instruction screen presented to participants is shown in Figure \ref{instructions}:

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \fbox{\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{instructions.png}}
        \caption{An example of an instruction screen provided during the experiment}
        \label{instructions}
    \end{figure}

    This was initially altered to provide more clarity (e.g. with wording such as \enquote{Task 1: Switch to image search} in a larger font), but the original version was shown to participants due to a Maze bug. 

    Another limitation related to experimental design was the decision to host the experiment online and asynchronously through Prolific. There are justifications for this choice (see Section \ref{3.1}) and precedent within both HCI and larger fields that feed into HCI \cite{jensen_does_2022, lu_understanding_2025}, but this choice also left us with noisy data before exclusions (see Section \ref{4.3}) and an astounding drop-off rate of $\sim38.9\%$.

    One of the main questions that arose after seeing the skewed proportion of dropped-off older versus younger users (see Table \ref{finish}, e.g. for $IO$, 6 18-35 users did not finish while 17 60+ users did not finish) was whether \enquote{tech-savvy} older users were being disproportionately represented in the dataset, as compared to \enquote{non-tech-savvy} older users. Sampling from a pool of already technologically-fluent users was another concern about sampling from Prolific (see Chapter \ref{3}).

    % The reasoning for not including dropped-off user responses within the dataset was that they would have contributed to the already high levels of noise within the dataset. That is to be expected, but when compounded against the noise that was derived from general user error in relation to the online and asynchronous nature of this study, it obscures the signal within heavily. A trend that was qualitatively observed was that users who dropped-off after making it to the UIs would \enquote{give up} after realizing they didn't get the answer immediately. This mirrors a phenomenon outlined in Mahmud et al.\@, where older users exhibit a larger amount of unproductive behaviors stemming from frustration due to obstruction from task-completion as compared to younger Age Groups \cite{mahmud_learning_2020}, which might explain the higher drop-off rate for older users as compared to younger.     The reasoning for not including dropped-off users' responses within the dataset was that incomplete sessions did not provide interpretable measures of task performance via $ReactionTime$ or $MisclickRate$. Indeed, task abandonment and behaviors indicative of user frustration (such as erratic mouse movements and random clicking) \cite{mahmud_learning_2020} are real-world behaviors of older users and are important to acknowledge. But once participants disengaged from the task, subsequent interaction patterns reflected abandonment behavior rather than purposeful navigation, and most importantly, left us with no measurable DV.
    
    In general, much of this could have been mitigated by conducting an in-person experiment. First and foremost, any percentage of the drop-off that was due to technical error would be eradicated. But with user error, in-person experiments can perform \enquote{quality control} on participants, which ensure their willingness to give a proper attempt, which despite the monetary reward, is not always the norm on online recruitment platforms such as Prolific. User error also could have been decreased with guidance from experiment leads when confusion arose during the tasks as well.

    The last limitation that will be acknowledged is tracking accuracy via $Misclick Rate$. This is due to its rapid growth at onset and plateau shortly after, which creates poles on both ends and skews reporting statistics such as means and medians. This does not negate its usefulness entirely, especially as a metric that provides insight into an additional dimension of task-completion (accuracy), but it certainly made Section \ref{4} less interpretable. It should be mentioned that this statistic is not without backing within the literature \cite{yu_reducing_2024}, but accuracy in general is not widely reported on as much in part because of an inability to find a more suitable alternative. For instance, converting $Misclick Rate$ into its numerator, the number of incorrect clicks, would still result in two tails, especially with the added phenomenon of random clicking due to frustration that was prevalent in this online study.

\section{Extensions}\label{5.3}
    One of the main lingering questions is whether or not this exciting interaction between $TO$ and 60+ is context-specific. The websites provided in this study were selected mainly for their everyday functionality and without more specific criteria for UI design (hence why $Website$ 3 had an incredibly complex UI as compared to the others). A future direction that could be pursued is replicating this study with a different set of UIs, or even a more curated sequence of interfaces. 

    Another main extension would be to replicate this study in-person. Achieving the same sample size would be difficult, but would be a worthy endeavor, especially given the limitations of online studies outlined above. This would also fall more in line with the standards for current HCI experimental design, but these will shift as fields that have adopted online studies more completely into their toolkits continue to influence the world of HCI.

    Some other directions that would be less important, but still interesting, to explore would be rotating around the order of appearance of the current UIs, and changing what type of icons are displayed (currently, flat icons are employed [see Section \ref{2.3.2}])-- it could be worth exploring implementing skeuomorphic iconography or alternative forms of iconography instead.

\section{Conclusion}\label{5.4}

    As technology continues to evolve at rapid, perhaps even unsustainable, rates \cite{chong_bad_2023}, the requirements of users need to be centered more than ever before. User-centric design is touted as the golden standard of product development in the technological sphere, but it is not an inherently all-inclusive framework. Instead, software development and UI/UX design typically centers the conventional user, someone with fluency in technological navigation and no lack in cognitive, visual, auditory, motor, etc.\@ abilities (see Section \ref{2.2}). This myth of the \enquote{everyday user} sidelines many people of varying ability levels, including user populations such as the elderly who experience unique combinations of disabilities associated with aging. 

    But there are reasons technologists return to the \enquote{everyday user} archetype time and time again. Accessibility can be a daunting topic, especially in the context of technology. Users require different modes of assistance, and hitting the goal post for every type of disability with a singular user interface can be an overwhelming ask. And the guides that do describe how to comprehensively promote accessibility on digital platforms, like the WCAG and ARIA \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005, putnam_it_2023}, are incredibly dense and long reads.

    But accessibility can also start small. In fact, icons are essentially the smallest unit of meaning on a technological interface. And even these tiny units of meaning can significantly and positively enhance the experience of older users in massive ways (think on the magnitude of seconds, even!).
