\chapter*{Abstract}
    In 2004, 70-98\% of websites were found to be inaccessible \cite{lazar_improving_2004}, and one of the user demographics most negatively affected by inaccessible websites is older users \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. Many Human-Computer Interaction researchers have looked at whether different variants of Internet icons create positive performance effects for older users, with generally inconclusive results. 
    
    The goal of this thesis is to measure whether different icon types (specifically text, icons, or icons with text labels) have a positive impact on older users's performance (as compared to younger users) in a web-based task-completion setting. Specifically, users were assigned to an icon type and then shown six unique websites each containing one task. This research was conducted online and asynchronously. A total of 240 users completed the experiment. Results were first examined as descriptive statistics and later thrown into a mixed-effects linear regression model to test for significance. The text-only condition came out triumphant, providing not only a boost in accuracy to both Age Groups, but specifically also a disproportionate boost in speed to the older user population.

\chapter*{Introduction}
    In 2004, 70-98\% of websites were found to be inaccessible \cite{lazar_improving_2004}, and one of the user demographics most negatively affected by inaccessible websites is older users \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. One subject that researchers in the field of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) study in order to create a more accessible Internet for older users is iconography. Even though semiotics has been a constant throughout human history (via languages), a unique lexicon of icons arose as the Internet developed, defined heavily by Western culture and the newly emerging, globalization-influenced Internet culture \cite{ma_semiotics_1995}, making a brand new barrier to entry for older user populations.

    Many HCI researchers have looked at whether different variants of Internet icons create positive performance effects for older users, but results have generally trended towards inconclusive. For instance, results varied on whether closer semantic distance, or “the closeness of meaning between icon and function” \cite{ganor_designing_nodate,liu_study_2023,dosso_how_2021,schroder_effects_2008}, or adding text labels \cite{wu_how_2022,dosso_how_2021,song_developing_2024} positively influences older user’s speeds and accuracies in ways significantly different than younger users. 

    Most of these studies were also conducted in cognitive psychology-inspired lab studies \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}– they remove the context of the web applications the icons would be embedded into in the real world. There are a couple of north star papers for this thesis that study icon accessibility within a more realistic context. Specifically, Yu \& Chattopadhyay provided some groundbreaking results \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. Yu \& Chattopadhyay presented 6 feature-rich mobile app user interfaces (UIs; e.g. Starbucks, AMC, Subway, etc.) and studied whether highlighting relevant features for the participants’s given task would boost performance speeds and accuracies among older adults\cite{yu_reducing_2024}. Shockingly, Yu \& Chattopadhyay found that with emphasized task-relevant features, older adults performed at the same speeds and accuracies during task completion as younger adults \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. But embedded, task-based environments have been far less explored and do not have the wealth of findings that controlled lab experiment iconography studies have.
    
    Causal findings and realism are trade-offs. Yet, given the large strides in technological advancement being pushed, it’s now more important than ever to tie together real-world applications into accessibility research. Alongside this, studying icons in the context of a realistic, task-based experimental design also could provide greater insight into these generally inconclusive findings. 
    
 	The goal of my experiment is to measure whether different icon types (specifically text, icons, or icons with text labels) have a positive impact on older users' performance (as compared to younger users) in a web-based task-completion setting. This experiment embedded these icons into 6 everyday web-based tasks, such as web browsing and food ordering. The research was conducted online and asynchronously. More specifically, participants were first recruited on Prolific, then redirected to a UX testing platform called Maze where the experiment was conducted, which plugged into Figma, a UI/UX design tool, to access these 6 webpages’ designs. Conducting experiments online a generally new procedure for many social sciences, and although HCI pioneers this push towards asynchronous research, accessibility research within HCI remains a stalwartly in support of in-person studies. With its novelty, a lot of scrutiny can be raised, which is addressed later in this paper in both Methodologies and Results, in sections \hyperlink{3}{3} and \hyperlink{4}{4}.
    
	A total of 240 users completed the experiment. Results were first examined as descriptive statistics and later thrown into a mixed-effects linear regression model to test for significance. The text-only condition came out significant, providing not only a boost in accuracy to both age groups, but specifically also a disproportionate boost in speed to the older user population, which is the main finding of this paper. Alongside this, the icon with text labels condition overall performs worse than the text-only and icon-only conditions.

    Although creating positive performance effects for both younger and older users is beneficial and should not be written off as completely null and void results, the exciting findings are the ones where older users are boosted is ways disproportionately greater than younger users. This is due to the idea of equity versus equality, where pushing for an equity-based design framework allows older users to access and experience technology in the same ways younger users do.

\chapter{Background Literature}

In the following chapter, I will discuss the literature motivating my thesis. I will first begin in section \hyperlink{1.1}{1.1} by discussing the multidisciplinary origins of human-computer interaction (HCI) and how these origins inform HCI's current-day initiatives. Then section \hyperlink{1.2}{1.2} will focus in on the HCI subfield of older user accessibility and the standards set for accessibility on the Internet. Section \hyperlink{1.3}{1.3} dives deeper into the academic research that drives these standards, especially regarding computer iconography, and finally, section \hyperlink{1.4}{1.4} discusses task-based research involving older users in HCI and summarizes the gap in the literature that my thesis aims to address.

\hypertarget{1.1}{\section{Human-Computer Interaction}}

        Human-computer interaction (HCI) was born in the 1960s out of the minds of a handful of computer scientists during a time where computers were \enquote{scarce, expensive, bulky and formal-scheduled machines used for automatic calculations} \cite{roussel_looking_nodate}. Their goal was to make computers more compatible with humans to \enquote{\textit{augment the human intellect}}, according to Douglas Engelbart, one of the founding fathers of HCI \cite{roussel_looking_nodate} (emphasis in original). With this clear focus in mind, graphical user interfaces, mouses, applications, and much more were developed \cite{myers_brief_1998}.

        In the 1980s, the tides began to turn as a wave of cognitive psychologists entered the field of HCI. With \enquote{[t]he body of knowledge, research findings and methods that made up cognitive psychology...} \cite{rogers_new_nodate}, HCI gained a robust toolkit and a broader, more holistic worldview.

        With the 1980's cognitive psychologists at the helm, the once narrowly-defined goal of HCI quickly expanded, leaving HCI \enquote{bursting at the seams} \cite{rogers_new_nodate}. Fast-forward to current day, HCI research topics includes everything from how individuals interact with their surrounding technology ecosystem (human-environment interactions), ethics in data privacy, alongside technology's potential in various sectors such as health, social governance, and education \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. 
        
        One of the biggest fields that HCI encompasses is accessibility, which \enquote{aims to ensure that IT applications and services can be used on an equal basis by users with disabilities and older users} \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. The need for expanded accessibility research cannot be overstated, as the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated that one in four people in the United States live with a disability \cite{putnam_it_2023}. Accessibility is a subfield that, even within a very diverse parent field, stands out for its multidisciplinary basis. Beyond computer science and psychology, many other fields also inform HCI's accessibility research, including user experience (UX) design \cite{putnam_it_2023}, ergonomics \cite{myers_brief_1998}, biology \cite{lindberg_how_2006, cruz_user-centered_2023}, and even economics \cite{nadkarni_task-based_2007}.

\hypertarget{1.2}{\section{Older User Accessibility}}
        As Stephanidis explicitly mentions, one of the key subjects of HCI accessibility research is older users \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. After all, the National Institute of Aging states that people over 60 are one of the fastest growing user groups on the Internet \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005}, and older users quite literally experience the Internet differently than younger users \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. This is due to age-related decline in vision \cite{ganor_designing_nodate, cruz_user-centered_2023, lindberg_how_2006, noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1}, which includes things like declines in color discrimination and narrowing of visual fields, memory \cite{ganor_designing_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1}, hearing \cite{noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1, cruz_user-centered_2023}, motility \cite{noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1, cruz_user-centered_2023, putnam_it_2023}, and more. Mahmud et al.~also reveal that even the exploration strategies older users employ on technological interfaces are different than other age groups \cite{mahmud_learning_2020}. It should be noted that despite all of the biological developments of aging, older people might not see themselves as \enquote{disabled}, which uniquely situates them in the wider accessibility discussion.

        Yet despite this overwhelming need for accessibility, according to a 2004 paper, 70-98\% of websites are not accessible \cite{lazar_improving_2004}. In order to ease the burden of encoding accessibility into websites, HCI researchers aim to update web accessibility standards to best match the needs of older users today.
        
        The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are hailed as \enquote{the epitome of Web accessibility guidelines} \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005}. The WCAG is targeted towards software developers, allowing them to weave accessibility even at the granularity of individual lines of code. Recent amendments to the Americans with Disabilities Act also make the WCAG the definitive standard for any institution receiving federal funding, which extends to governmental organizations, higher education establishments, and more \cite{ada_fact_2024}. Other widely-used accessibility standards include Accessible Rich Internet Applications (ARIA), which is derived from UX standards \cite{putnam_it_2023}, and Section 508 of the U.S.'s Rehabilitation Act (29 U.S.C. 795d), which puts forth legal requirements for accessibility within American information and communication technology \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005, noauthor_section508gov_nodate, noauthor_section508gov_nodate-1, henry2006}. Other countries have also published their own guidelines on web accessibility \cite{kurniawan_research-derived_2005}. 

\hypertarget{1.3}{\section{Older Users and Icons}}
        But what informs these accessibility standards? Behind the scenes, HCI accessibility researchers have kept pace with the ever-changing realm of technology, maintaining up-to-date standards even when innovation turns web accessibility on its head. One of these aspects of technology that has undergone substantial development in the mere decades since the inception of the Internet is icons. 
        
        Iconography is a constant in human history. Semiotics, which studies icons, understands them as the foundation of human communication systems, with meanings that are socially and culturally constructed \cite{ma_semiotics_1995}. As the World Wide Web (WWW) emerged, its graphical interface naturally brought rise to the WWW's very own lexicon of iconography, defined primarily by Western culture and the newly emerging, globalization-influenced Internet culture \cite{myers_brief_1998, ma_semiotics_1995, knight_cultural_2009}. And as icons have emerged as a new mechanism of expressing meaning on the Internet, whose culture ever-fluctuates, HCI researchers have begun delving into the various facets of this brand new virtual iconography.

    \hypertarget{1.3.1}{\subsection{Semantic Distance}}
        One of these areas of interest is semantic distance, which is defined as \enquote{the closeness of meaning between icon and function} \cite{ganor_designing_nodate}. Results are varied on whether closer semantic distance has a significant positive influence on older users' speeds and accuracy. 

        Liu et al.~studied the impact of semantic distance (concrete versus abstract) and icon design (skeuomorphic versus flat, which is discussed in \hyperlink{1.3.2}{1.3.2}) on older versus younger participants' reaction times \cite{liu_study_2023}. Specifically, Liu et al.~ended up studying four different icon types-- skeuomorphic concrete, flat concrete, skeuomorphic abstract, and flat abstract. They aimed to measure the resulting cognitive load of participants, which was quantified via performance measures such as reaction time (RT), physiological measures (pupil diameter and the number of fixations), and subjective assessment measures. Participants were given semantic words, then were asked to judge whether the icon shown after the word matched. After analysis via a 3-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA), they found that when icons had further semantic distance \enquote{older participants had significantly higher reaction times... than younger participants} \cite{liu_study_2023}. 
        
        Dosso and Chevalier also found similar results in their study of semantic distance (close versus far) and text labels (icon versus label versus combination, which will be discussed in \hyperlink{1.3.3}{1.3.3}) on different age groups (young versus old) \cite{dosso_how_2021}. This study measured RTs (both including rereading and without rereading), correct response scores, and the number and length of rereads when presenting users with 30 interfaces of icons. Within the interface, there were 20 icons total, 19 being distractors and 1 being the target, the only study out of the ones in \hyperlink{1.3}{1.3} that shows 20 icons in an icon selection-based task (most opt for 3x3 arrays, as seen in \cite{wu_how_2022, xi_influence_2018, song_developing_2024}. Through ANOVAs performed per dependent variable, Dosso and Chevalier concluded that \enquote{older users needed more time to select icons, specifically when semantic distance was far} \cite{dosso_how_2021}. 
        
        However, the sureness of these findings have been contested, such as by Ganor and Te'eni \cite{ganor_designing_nodate} and Schröder and Ziefle \cite{schroder_effects_2008}. Ganor and Te'eni observed whether differences in an icon's level of detail (simple versus detailed) and semantic distance (close versus distant) created any significant effects on different age groups' (young versus old) performance time and performance accuracy \cite{ganor_designing_nodate}. They achieved this by setting forth 40 tasks, where each task had participants choose the target icon amongst 7 other distractors, which, like Dosso and Chevalier, is an uncommon array format \cite{dosso_how_2021}. Although they did find a greater negative impact of semantic distance on user performance on older participants, this impact was not significant according to their ANOVA analyses. 
        
        Schröder and Ziefle also looked at similar independent variables, manipulating icon concreteness and semantic distance (concrete versus abstract) and complexity (simple versus complex) on different age group's (young versus old) RT and fraction of confirmatory responses per item (CR) \cite{schroder_effects_2008}. This resulted in 4 types of icons, namely abstract simple, abstract complex, concrete simple, and concrete complex. Schröder and Ziefle specifically showed mobile phone icons and gave 4 targets and 4 \enquote{dummies} per task for participants to select from-- this is the only study in \hyperlink{1.3}{1.3} that uses more than one \enquote{dummy}/distractor icon per task (see \cite{dosso_how_2021, ganor_designing_nodate} and more). Through a 3-way repeated measures ANOVA, they found that although there was no significant effect of semantic distance on RT, there was a significant effect on CR, and still argued that concreteness is a more crucial factor than complexity when designing icons for elderly users in an inconclusive manner \cite{schroder_effects_2008}. 
        
        Generally, the consensus is that semantically close icons elicit faster reaction speeds, but this has not been confirmed to benefit elderly users more so than the average user in a significant manner.

    \hypertarget{1.3.2}{\subsection{Skeuomorphic Design}}

        As mentioned in Liu et al.\@, the design of icons have also been investigated extensively with somewhat mixed results \cite{liu_study_2023}. Specifically, skeuomorphic icons are of great interest to HCI researchers. Skeuomorphic icons, or skeuomorphisms, are icons that \enquote{are usually designed with 3D effect graphics to imitate real objects through gradients, textures, highlights and shadows, and other visual features to bring users a realistic feeling to help them better understand the interface} \cite{liu_study_2023}. These are usually studied in conjunction with flat icons, both of which you can see in Figure \hyperlink{skeuo}{1.1} below. Liu et al.\@, which as mentioned above had independent variables of semantic distance and skeuomorphic icon design \cite{liu_study_2023}, and Wu et al.\@, which studied skeuomorphisms in connection to text labels, among other conditions \cite{wu_how_2022}, both found that skeuomorphisms had a positive impact on older users' reaction speeds. 

        \begin{figure}[H]
            \hypertarget{skeuo}{}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{thesistemplate_2020-04-24/figures/skeuo.png}
            \caption{From Liu et al.~\cite{liu_study_2023}, examples of (a) Skeuomorphic Concrete, (b) Flat Concrete, (c) Flat Abstract, and (d) Skeuomorphic Abstract icons (reprinted by permission of Informa UK Limited, trading as Taylor \& Francis Group, \hyperlink{https://www.tandfonline.com}{https://www.tandfonline.com})}
        \end{figure}
        
        Specifically, Wu et al.~looked at icon type (skeuomorphic icon and text versus flat icon and text versus skeuomorphic icon versus flat icon) and cognitive level on search accuracy, RT, and time to first fixation (the time it takes before glancing at the target icon) \cite{wu_how_2022}. Participants were all 60-66 years old and based in Hangzhou. They were asked to look at 3x3 arrays of icons, with 8 distractors and 1 target, all comprised of icons seen in medical services technology in the regions of Beijing, Shanghai, and Hangzhou. Skeuomorphisms elicited the highest search accuracies, fastest RTs, and fastest times to first fixation (with the order of icon types from best to worst being skeuomorphic icons and text, flat icons and text, skeuomorphic icon, flat icon) \cite{wu_how_2022}. These effects seemed to be the results of reduced semantic distance via realistic design. 

        But other studies, such as Xi and Wu, found that flat icons, as opposed to skeuomorphic icons, had high search performance within older participant groups \cite{xi_influence_2018}. This was found in a study centered around icon style (line versus metro versus skeuomorphism versus flat) and its effect on total visit duration (per icon) and eye movement contrail (the moving line of sight during a task). Notably, this study's participant group were all graduate students, unlike all other studies within \hyperlink{1.3}{1.3} and \hyperlink{1.4}{1.4}'s readings. However, like Wu et al.\@, icons were arranged in a 3x3 grid, with the same ratio of distractors to target icons \cite{wu_how_2022}. After ANOVA analysis, they found that the total visit duration for flat icons was significantly shorter than skeuomorphic icons. Xi and Wu suggested could be due to the higher cognitive load which comes from deciphering the highly-detailed skeuomorphic designs. 

        Most results point towards skeuomorphic icons over flat icons for elderly-friendly design, however Xi and Wu creates greater nuance in this conversation \cite{xi_influence_2018}, since positive effects elicited by certain design choices to elderly users are typically also experienced by younger users.

    \hypertarget{1.3.3}{\subsection{Icons and Text Labels}}

        A subtopic within accessible iconography that might be the most inconclusive as of now is the impact of text labels. Previously mentioned studies such as Dosso and Chevalier \cite{dosso_how_2021} and Wu et al.~\cite{wu_how_2022}, among others, show that older users perform faster when presented icons with text labels as opposed to icons alone. However, Dosso and Chevalier state that this positive impact was experienced equally by both younger and older age groups \cite{dosso_how_2021}, which is seconded by Song et al.\@'s findings \cite{song_developing_2024}. 
        
        Song et al.\@'s study studied not only text labels, but also icon style (similar, symbolic, example, arbitrary), which mashes together the explorations of semantic distance (\hyperlink{1.3.1}{1.3.1}) and icon design (\hyperlink{1.3.2}{1.3.2}) \cite{song_developing_2024}. Participants of different ages (young versus old) were measured in RT and accuracy in 72 tasks which, like Liu et al.\@, first showed the semantic name, then showed a 3x3 array of 1 target and 8 distractor icons \cite{liu_study_2023}, like Wu et al.~and Xi and Wu \cite{wu_how_2022, xi_influence_2018}. According to a 2-way ANOVA, the RT of older participants increased across all icon types when text labels were provided, and most exciting of all, older and younger accuracies were equivalent after adding text labels to the icons (which without the text labels were not) \cite{song_developing_2024}. 

        Islam approaches this subtopic of elderly accessibility design in a slightly unconventional manner \cite{islam_exploring_2015}. Islam still conducts a laboratory study, but instead of providing analysis just via quantitative measures, Islam centers their behavioral study around semi-structured interviews. Specifically, after sourcing icons from 2 web domain interfaces, namely a museum webpage and a university webpage, they show the icons to the participants and ask them a series of both open-ended, probing, and closed questions, resulting in interviews between 100 to 120 minutes long. These questions included \enquote{What could be the referential meaning [of the icon]?} and \enquote{How confident are you that you are correct?} \cite{islam_exploring_2015}. Islam's participants were largely graduate students, but not entirely graduate students like \cite{xi_influence_2018}, with 22/28 being graduate students, 5/28 being full-time company workers, and 1/28 being research assistants. Through qualitative and quantitative analyses, Islam found that \enquote{the textual interface signs showed better performance in 4 groups; iconic signs showed better performance in 3 groups; and icon[s] with text sign[s] showed better performance in [the] remaining 4 groups} \cite{islam_exploring_2015}, pointing towards no definite right choice for whether text labels on icons are more accessible for older users. 
        
        However, these 3 dimensions of text-label-icon interaction have not been widely studied, so my research hopes to further add to this budding discussion on the impacts of text labels on icon accessibility for older users.

\hypertarget{1.4}{\section{Older Users and Task-Based Experimental Design}}
        The vast majority of the studies cited in section \hyperlink{1.3}{1.3} are modeled after traditional cognitive psychology methodology, recording reaction times to stimuli presented in isolation without the context of an actual website surrounding said stimuli \cite{stephanidis_seven_2019}. Isolating the icons does reap strong causal conclusions, but in the vast, virtual universe of the Internet, are icons ever shown in isolation? Causal findings and realism are trade-offs, and it is my belief that, given the previously inconclusive laboratory experiments on older users and iconography, studying icons in the context of a realistic, task-based experimental design would provide greater additional insight.

        And research on the negative impacts of website clutter on older user performance speeds further this point. Nadkarni in \cite{nadkarni_task-based_2007} phrases it best: \enquote{perceived website complexity (PWC) is central to understanding how sophisticated features of a website... affect a visitor's experience}. Grahame et al.~defines clutter as pixels as a percentage of the available page space that was occupied by text or graphics \cite{grahame_age_2004}. This was primarily studied by controlling for the number of links on a webpage, but Grahame et al.~also manipulated the font size of the links, the placement of the target link on the UI, etc.. The two age groups (young versus old) were shown web pages modeled off of free use templates and were recorded for number of errors, RT, and fixation number. Through ANOVAs calculated per dependent variable, Grahame et al.~determined that increased clutter significantly increased search times and increased error rates for older adults \cite{grahame_age_2004}. 

        And although HCI leans towards laboratory studies, there is a longstanding history of task-based procedural research. One of the earliest examples of this can be found in 1993, where the motivation of studying tasks women were \enquote{more likely to perform... in the real world} \cite{czaja_age_1993}, Czaja and Sharit studied how age impacted performance speeds for women, ages 25-75 years old, across 3 different tasks-- namely, data entry, file modification, and inventory management. Data entry was simple and repetitive, requiring the participants to change addresses according to instruction cards. File modification was an intermediary level task, where participants were given a database of high-schoolers organized by grade level and asked to check for consistency between individual student records and instruction cards. Finally, inventory management was the least structured and most demanding task, requiring participants to track the inventory of products from outside suppliers and manipulate this relational database according to instruction cards. Czaja and Sharit showed that increased age, as well as computer experience, were the greatest determiners of faster response times and a smaller number of errors across all 3 tasks \cite{czaja_age_1993}. 
        
        In Mahmud et al.\@, three age groups were studied, each with 10 participants per group: children, adults, and older adults \cite{mahmud_learning_2020}. However, this study takes a different approach than Czaja and Sharit-- Mahmud et al.~instead asked these 3 age groups, who all were first-time users of Microsoft's OneNote, to complete 4 main tasks and observed their exploratory styles \cite{mahmud_learning_2020, czaja_age_1993}. These tasks were complex, multistep tasks, including making a new notebook, adding relevant notes to a notebook, and sharing notebooks with friends, etc.. After coding video footage of the participants using OneNote and analyzing pre- and post-exploration interviews, Mahmud et al.~found a great treasure trove of findings on older users in embedded, task-based exploration settings. For instance, as many of the other readings mentioned may suggest \cite{liu_study_2023, dosso_how_2021, grahame_age_2004}, older participants are overall slower at task completion as compared to other age groups. Their exploration style tended to not fully explore all options, rely on previous experience with other UIs to their detriment, and experience more breakdowns when faced with errors. In fact, older adults typically spent a longer time repeating sequences of failed selections, whereas adults and children more often redirected their efforts based on error messaging. And generally, Mahmud et al.~found that older participants were generally dissatisfied with self-exploration, whereas both children and adults maintained high satisfaction levels and confidence before and after the study. 

        The study that inspired my thesis the most is Yu and Chattopadhyay's 2024 study of mobile tasks within the context of complex, feature-rich UIs \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. This experiment involved multiple phases, first beginning with a young (30 participants) versus old (30 participants) comparison of task completion given 4 relevant feature highlighting techniques, where weighted zoom and highlight with context prevailed. The final phase was implementing an AI-assistant to reduce the search space powered by OpenAI, NavNudge, which exhibited 94\% accuracy for their \textit{Query} dataset and 93\% accuracy for their \textit{UILabel} dataset. Yu presented the UI of a map application none of the participants (who were all older (10 participants), none overlapping with the first phase of the study) were familiar with and studied whether providing an AI assistant (NavNudge) which could decipher speech prompts and highlight (via weighted zoom or highlight with context, as determined by the 1st part of this study) the top 3 most relevant features based on the participant's given task would boost performance speeds and accuracies among older adults. For example, an example of a task would be \enquote{Find out how long it will take to travel from Chicago O’Hare Airport to Chicago Union Station using public transit.} \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. Specifically, older adults were first presented with the UI without visual cues, and if they remained stuck on the given task for more than 13 seconds or willingly gave up, they were able to use NavNudge to recover. Shockingly, Yu and Chattopadyay found that with emphasized task-relevant features, older adults performed at the same speeds and accuracies during task completion as younger adults \cite{yu_reducing_2024}. And it wasn't that the older adults were not getting tripped up as easily, it was that with the help of NavNudge, these older adults were recovering successfully, as opposed to the behaviors exhibited by older adults when they encountered errors in Mahmud et al.~\cite{mahmud_learning_2020}.
        
        If this decomplexification effort can be replicated with icons, whose purpose is to use condensed symbols to represent larger meanings, especially when studied in a task-based experimental design, this would be monumental. As seen in the HCI research above, it is exceedingly rare to find design changes that allow older users and younger users to perform as equals. If substantiated, these findings could introduce a brand new paradigm of iconography and older user accessibility.

        
\chapter{Methodology}

This chapter discusses the methodology of this study. As an overview, this study was conducted online and asynchronously. I used a participant matching platform called Prolific for recruitment, and then redirected participants to Maze, a UX usability testing platform, to measure their speeds and accuracies in completing 6 tasks embedded within web interfaces. 

In this chapter, Section \hyperlink{2.1}{2.1} first justifies the use of Prolific for participant recruitment, especially in light of recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) and its potential harm towards sound online data collection. Then, Section \hyperlink{2.2}{2.2} runs through the procedure on Prolific and Maze and discusses the design choices behind the Figma UI wireframes used in the procedure. Finally, Section \hyperlink{2.3}{2.3} discusses the data analysis strategy for the quantitative (speed and accuracy.)

\hypertarget{2.1}{\section{Prolific and Online Survey Platforms}}
    Prolific, as the introduction to this chapter states, is an online recruitment platform. It allows researchers to post online, asynchronous studies to the platform for its exceptionally large user base to participate in. Surveys can gather large samples in a matter of hours or even minutes. However, the reliability of the platform has seen significant doubt as AI makes rapid gains in its ability to make autonomous decisions and actions.

\hypertarget{2.1.2}{\subsection{In Favor of Online Survey Platforms}}
    
    I begin by introducing a quote from a comparative analysis of Prolific, which at the time was an up-and-coming alternative to the long-used Mechanical Turk (MTurk) from Amazon: 
        \enquote{The success of online experiments is not surprising, as they offer at-scale recruitment of participants in a short time, are generally cheap, and offer access to a broader population – potentially even representative of the internet population – than classical lab experiments.} \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}
        
    And these benefits are especially exciting in the elderly accessibility area of HCI, where recruitment of older participants is especially difficult. For instance, Yu and Chattopadhyay performed a power analysis using one study to estimate the expected effect size, which resulted in a total of 60 participants \cite{yu_reducing_2024}, and Mahmud et al.\@ had only 30 total participants \cite{mahmud_learning_2020} -- and as a reminder, these studies are further divided by age group, with Yu and Chattopadhyay having older and younger subgroups and Mahmud et al.\@ having a children/adults/older adults 3-way split. And although the results in these papers are exciting, with such limited sample sizes, it's hard to ensure the ability to generalize findings and the adequately representative diversity of these samples. This is mitigated with Prolific, which has a total \enquote{active, verified, and engaged} participant base of over 200,000 users \cite{run_prolific}.
    
    \enquote{[G]enerally cheap} is also a key benefit, but it's of note that Prolific pays an ethical wage, unlike other platforms such as MTurk, which is known for \enquote{unreasonably low subject payment} \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}. Specifically, Prolific ensures a minimum fixed payment per unit of time, calculated based on a minimum hourly reward of \$8 U.S. Dollars (USD), with a recommended hourly reward of \$12 USD \cite{how_prolific}. 

% might not actually wanna add... still kind of low lol
%And even though the sample size for this study goes significantly beyond what has previously been done in this field (200 total, 100 per age group), compensating each individual fairly does not break the bank-- even with the \$12 USD recommended rate, given the short estimated length of this experiment (5 minutes), each person gets \$1 USD for participating, which with Prolific fees ended up costing \$266.40 USD.

    Online survey platforms such as Prolific are no silver bullet, though. There's longstanding belief that these participants, in order to make cash out quick, speed through experiments to earn their incentive and move on to the next survey \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}. This fear is only amplified with the onset of AI in an online survey context. Peterson and Asher et al.\@ both studied AI-generated free responses on MTurk and Prolific respectively \cite{peterson_impact_nodate, asher_chatbots_2025}. Peterson found that just 20\% of the 1443 responses passed quality checks and counted as usable data \cite{peterson_impact_nodate}, whereas Asher et al.'s findings on Prolific was a little more hopeful-- when using a keystroke tool to flag submissions with an unusually low number of keystrokes, they found that only 9\% were AI-assisted responses \cite{asher_chatbots_2025}. But generally, MTurk is more vulnerable to AI responses \cite{christoforou_generative_2024}, partially due to its unethical wages, which encourages long-tail exploitation that oftentimes isn't valuable enough to humans to engage in \cite{carlini_llms_2025}. And now, with the rise of commercially-available AI models that can easily execute computer tasks, such as OpenAI's Operator \cite{openai_operator}, Claude's Sonnet 3.0 \cite{claude_sonnet}, and Google's Gemini 2.5 \cite{google_gemini}, things look even more bleak for online survey models such as Prolific. It is important to note that no real research has been done on the topic of so-called \enquote{computer use} AI models participating in online surveys yet.

    Luckily, Prolific takes measures against AI and participants who rush through studies. Prolific allows researchers to manually approve study participants for payment, which can help researchers screen for participants with unusually fast completion times or other suspicious activity \cite{prolific_home}. The platform, during the writing of this paper, is beta-ing an option to automatically \enquote{[r]eject exceptionally fast submissions}, which also deters against these bad actors \cite{prolific_home}. But none of these absolutely destroy the threat. In contemplating trade-offs, both via reading the literature presented above and discussing with Reed faculty and staff (see my \hyperlink{ACKs}{Acknowledgments}), the ability to seamlessly recruit a notably difficult demographic at such large numbers outweighed the threat of falsified data. If anything, by bulking up the sample size, it mitigates the effect of a singular, or even a handful of AI-responses, if any. But this is not to say that the risk is zero, which is a hard truth kept front-of-mind throughout the following phases of this thesis. 

    And as briefly touched upon above, another concern lies in whether the results will be overall skewed faster than average because some online survey users tend to rush through surveys to earn more, and that generally these users will be more fluent in computer technology given that they actively participate in online survey platforms such as Prolific. This concern is valid and real, however, this is a bias that could have just as easily occurred in an in-person study as well. To gather a significant number of older users, one could suggest recruiting them in community spaces elderly folks frequent, such as the library and senior living communities, but people who willingly participant may be more confident and skilled in computer-use than the average older user (although perhaps not to the same extent). And in terms of diversity, online recruitment wins over local recruitment. 

    All in all, Prolific is not the perfect recruitment tool. But in order to get significant results and to ensure smooth recruitment and data collection, this study has decided to use Prolific to meet its ends.

There are various platforms of this nature, including Playbook UX, Userbrain, Lookback, etc., but Maze came out on top in terms of pricing, study set-up options, and recorded data types, amongst other features \cite{unmoderated_user-testing_tools-nng_nodate}. \hypertarget{2.3}{\section{Data Analysis}}
    \hypertarget{2.3.1}{\subsection{Data Recording and Migration}} 
        All data was recorded by Maze and hand-replicated within an Excel spreadsheet. The spreadsheet was formatted such that each participant had six entries, one per website. Each entry contained the following information: 
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item $Maze_ID$ (a string of integers)
            \item $Prolific_ID$ (a string of integers and characters)
            \item $Age_Group$ (either 18-35 or 60+)
            \item $Icon_Type$ (either Text Only, Icon Only, or Icon with Text Labels)
            \item $Website$ (an integer between 1-6 corresponding to the order of appearance within the experiment's user flow)
            \item $Speed$ (floating point number, in seconds)
            \item $Misclick_Rate$ (floating point number, represented as a percentage of wrong clicks over total number of clicks)
        \end{enumerate}

        User responses were only recorded if they had fully completed the study, meaning that they found correct solutions to all six websites. Users who did not complete all six websites or dropped out before being assigned to a condition (which is an important distinction for \hyperlink{2.3.3}{later} in this section) were not included within the final Excel form.
        
        The spreadsheet ended up containing 1440 total rows, corresponding to 240 unique participant answers. In order to process the spreadsheet information via \textit{pandas}, a Python data analysis library (which will be discussed further in \hyperlink{2.3.2}{2.3.2} below), the Excel spreadsheet was first converted into a \textit{.csv} file.

    \hypertarget{2.3.2}{\subsection{Tech Stack}}

        All data analysis was performed in Python, enabled by various Python libraries. The code for my data analysis, as well as the anonymized \textit{.csv} file mentioned in \hyperlink{2.3.1}{2.3.1}, can be found in the Github repository here: \url{https://github.com/00eemsy/thes}.
    
        The primary Python library employed was \textit{pandas}, which is stylized in all lowercase, is a popular, open-source data analysis library \cite{pandas}. This was used to access the data within the \textit{.csv} file, clean up the data to remove empty rows and columns, and to perform data manipulation required for both the descriptive statistics and actual linear regression (see below in \hyperlink{2.3.3}{2.3.3} and \hyperlink{2.3.4}{2.3.4}). For instance, \textit{pandas} was employed in the descriptive statistics to calculate the average speed of participants per website.
    
        \textit{Matplotlib} is another popular, open-source Python library used instead for data visualization \cite{matplotlib}. All of the data visualizations, from bar charts, histograms, to scatterplots (which can all be found in the Appendix), were constructed by \textit{Matplotlib} with \textit{pandas} data. 
    
        Besides these main two librares, several other libraries were employed in one-off cases, including \textit{scipy} (lowercase intentional) for chi-squared value calculations \cite{scipy_chisquare} and \textit{numpy} for array manipulation in tandem with \textit{pandas} \cite{numpy}.

    \hypertarget{2.3.3}{\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}}
        Performing descriptive statistics analysis before the formal linear regression was necessary to achieve multiple goals. The main goal of these descriptive statistics, besides creating some casual familiarity with the data, was determining exclusion criteria. As mentioned above in \hyperlink{2.1}{2.1}, one of the biggest drawbacks to online survey platforms is noisy data. This can be caused by participants speeding through instructions \cite{palan_prolificacsubject_2018}, randomly clicking through UX tests, or even AI bot inaccuracies \cite{peterson_impact_nodate, asher_chatbots_2025, christoforou_generative_2024} (see more in \hyperlink{2.1.1}{2.1.1}). So cleaning up data is a priority to distill the data points into something potentially of meaning.

        The second goal of these descriptive statistics was making sense of the drop-off rate for those assigned an $Icon_Type$ variant. As mentioned in \hyperlink{2.3.1}{2.3.1}, this is related, but distinct, from drop-off rate for those not assigned an Icon Type variant and total drop-off rate. Specifically their relationship is as follows:

        $$\text{Total Drop-off} = \text{Drop-off}_{\text{Assigned Icon Type}} + \text{Drop-off}_{\text{Not Assigned Icon Type}} $$
        
        Maze recorded a total of 393 unique attempts, which compared to the 240 successfully completed attempts, was a cause for concern given the $\sim38.9\%$ total drop-off rate. To this end, descriptive statistics were supposed to give some insight into what could have possibly created this large total drop-off rate, and whether this had something to do either with the areas of interest (mainly, Age Group and Icon Type). Hence, this is why we look specifically at $\text{Drop-off}_{\text{Assigned Icon Type}}$.

        In terms of goal one, most of the work was looking at the dependent variables (DV; Speed, Misclick Rate) in relation to the various independent variables (IV; Age Group, Website, Icon Type, etc.). Average Speed was considered both per Website and summed over all 6 websites. To help contextualize these calculations, standard deviation was also derived to understand the variance within these datasets and point towards potential significance. More detailed numbers will be discussed in the Results section below. 

        This data was then plotted in various manners to help visualize the information. The three main vehicles to visualize this data were bar charts (for visualizing IV/DV correlations), histograms (for plotting distributions of DVs), and scatterplots (to compare between Speed and Misclick Rate). 

        As for $\text{Drop-off}_{\text{Assigned Icon Type}}$ calculations, the main route for analysis was a chi-square test of independence. A chi-square test of independence decides if two variables are related \cite{discovery_chi-square}. This was performed between the number of participants per Age Group and Icon Type who finished the experiment versus those who did not finished but were assigned a variant. To be absolutely clear, those who dropped off before making it to the websites were not included in this measure. 

    \hypertarget{2.3.4}{\subsection{Mixed Effects Linear Regression}}

    Many hypothesis testing methods were considered before settling on a mixed effects linear regression. First, a simpler model of testing was considered, namely a t-test. These are ideal for testing between two conditions, but given that per t-test there is a 5\% false positive error rate per test that compounds \cite{one-way}, comparing between more than two conditions (given the three Icon Type variants) was not viable. 

    One-way ANOVAs were also considered, mainly because of their frequent appearance in the literature \cite{ganor_designing_nodate, schroder_effects_2008, liu_study_2023, dosso_how_2021, wu_how_2022, xi_influence_2018, song_developing_2024, grahame_age_2004, yu_reducing_2024}, as a means to analyze data. And to some extent, an ANOVA is simply a type of linear regression. However, ANOVAs perform mean comparison between categorical variables, whereas a regular linear regression discovers relationships between continuous variable, which was more necessary given that Speed and Misclick Rate are continuous variables.

    Finally, the exact formulation of the linear regression has changed over time. Originally, the formula was:
    $$Outcome\sim Age + Icon + Website +(Age \times Icon)$ in quasi-R notation and $DV = \beta_0 + \beta_1(Age_{60+})+(\beta_2(Icon_{IWTL})+\beta_3(Icon_{TO}))+(\beta_4(Website_2)+\beta_5(Website_3)+\beta_6(Website_4) + \beta_7(Website_5) + \beta_8(Website_6)) + \epsilon$$ 
    
    This equation measures outcome (either Speed or Misclick Rate) from the main effects of Age Group, Icon Type, Website, and the interaction term between Age Group and Icon Type. However, this misses a key random effect, namely that of Participant, since various aspects of a Participant cannot be controlled for (Age Group, Icon Type, etc.)

    The final equation required a mix of both fixed and random effect variables, hence Mixed Effects Linear Regression. In quasi-R-speak, this equation is $Outcome\sim Age + Icon + Website +(Age \times Icon) + (1|Participant)$, which is equivalent to

    \begin{gather*}
        DV_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(Age_{60+})_i+(\beta_2(Icon_{IWTL})+\beta_3(Icon_{TO}))_i+\\(\beta_4(Website_2)+\beta_5(Website_3)+\beta_6(Website_4) + \beta_7(Website_5) + \beta_8(Website_6))_j + u_i + \epsilon_{ij}
    \end{gather*}

    This is later amended to the following after applying exclusion criteria (which is explained more in \hyperlink{2.3.4}{2.3.4}):

    \begin{gather*}
        DV_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(Age_{60+})_i+(\beta_2(Icon_{IWTL})+\beta_3(Icon_{TO}))_i+\\(\beta_4(Website_4) + \beta_5(Website_5) + \beta_6(Website_6))_j + u_i + \epsilon_{ij}
    \end{gather*}
    
    The $u_i$ represents the Participant random effect. The $i$ indexes an individual participant, whereas $j$ indexes by website, meaning $j\in [1,6]$. The indices correlate to the two levels of observation, namely the participant level (which include Age Group, Icon Type) and the within-participants level (since each person provides more than one (specifically, 6) outcome.)

    With so many terms, overfitting was a risk. Overfitting occurs when terms are added to a model to the point that the model no longer represents the generalizable phenomenon and instead the specific dataset instead \cite{perraillon}. Adding terms always makes for a better fit, but there's a fine line between increasing accuracy in a truly beneficial manner and in an inorganic manner.
    
    In order to avoid this, an additive approach was employed, meaning that each term was added onto the model and then compared while building them up. Model 0 was simply modeling the results, model 1 added in Age Group, model 2 added in Icon Type, model 3 added in Website, and finally, model 4 added in the Age Group/Icon Type interaction term. Furthermore, the performance metric for regression models employed (Akaike Information Criterion) also further negates this risk, which will be discussed later.

    In order to compare model fitness, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was employed. AIC for linear regressions is:

    $$AIC_p = n\times \ln\left(\frac{SSE_p}{n}\right)+2p$$

    The parameters of AIC are as follows: $p$ is the number of estimated parameters, $n$ is the number of observations, and $SSE$ is the residual sum of squares. Essentially, this statistic estimates the amount of information lost when using a certain model \cite{aic}. That being said, the lowest AIC retained the most information, meaning it was the more accurate out of the compared models. The bigger the drops in AIC between models, the more influential that specific effect was in producing the data. 

    There are several metrics for comparing performance between regression models, including $R^2$, but $AIC$ was the best choice because it penalized overfitting and balances goodness of fit and parsimony well \cite{perraillon}. Specifically, the $2p$ penalizes having a larger number of parameters \cite{perraillon}, which makes it better for combating overfitting as compared to other statistics such as $R^2$.
    
    When calculating AIC, several pairs of models were tested. The difference in AIC between models 1 and 0 was looked at to assess the impact of Age Group on the model. Models 2 and 1 were compared via AIC to estimate Icon Type's influence, models 3 and 2 estimated Website's influence, and finally, models 4 and 3 assess the importance of the Age Group/Icon Type interaction term. Most important for our study was seeing how the interaction between Age Group and Icon Type affected the model's fitness. 

    After deriving the most fit model, the significance levels (via p-values) were assessed of various variables compared to \enquote{baseline} variables (which were chosen by default by \textit{statsmodel}). Through these p-values, true significance was determined.

    This Mixed Effects Linear Model approach was employed on a plethora of exclusion criteria, which were determined via descriptive statistics, which are discussed above in \hyperlink{2.3.3}{2.3.3}. Tests were done with the following exclusions:
    
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item None
        \item Websites 1 and 3
        \item Average Misclick Rate $>80\%$
        \item Total Speed $>200$s
        \item Total Speed $>200$s or Average Misclick Rate $>80\%$
    \end{enumerate}

\hypertarget{4}{\chapter{Discussion}}

    This experiment was intricate and littered with learning many curves for the Computer Science-trained thesis author. That being said, there is a lot to discuss. 
    
    Generally, the format of this chapter will be as follows: first, qualitative conjectures will be attached to quantitative results found above in \hyperlink{3}{chapter 3}, a section that will be labeled as Interpretations. Second, limitations will be brought to light, confronted, and acknowledged. Then, future directions will be suggested, with a resounding conclusion to end this paper.

\hypertarget{4.1}{\section{Interpretations}}
    Two main results follow from \hyperlink{3}{Results}, namely that the Text-Only condition has a disproportionately positive influence on the 60+ Age Group (and that TO overall performs better across Age Groups than IO and IWTL), and that the Icon with Text Labels condition overall performs worse than TO and IO conditions. A reminder that these results hold for Websites 2, 4-6, which will be discussed later on in this chapter.

\hypertarget{4.1.1}{\subsection{Text-Only and 60+ Interpretations}}
    To reiterate, when modeling Speed, the best model of fit was the regression that included Age Group x Icon Type interaction terms. Within the calculations for this model, the interaction term between the 60+ Age Group and the TO Icon Type conditions had a coefficient of -3.018 and a p-value of 0.018. In other words, the 60+ Age Group averaged 3.018 second less on tasks that were shown in the TO condition than the 18-35 group.

    As for Misclick Rate and TO, in both semi-complete model 3 and fully-expanded model 4, TO by itself had significance (p-values=0.001, 0.037) both with negative coefficients (coefficients=-8.351, -7.813). The interaction term from above was not significant.

    These findings, especially the significance of the TO/60+ interaction in relation to Speed, are huge. As explained in the Background Literature, one of the main focuses of the age-based accessibility subfield of HCI is finding ways to even the technological playing field for older users experiencing age-related physical and mental declines. Of course, finding accessibility fixes that both Age Groups benefit from is also exciting, and in practice is what will be employed in building technology but the grand prize is discovering tweaks that overall benefit all ages but that older users happen to also respond better to than younger users. It's in line with notions of equality versus equity-- one makes sure all are given equal resources, and the other ensures all are given adequate resources to even the playing field. 

    This result finds backing in scholarship, as well. As mentioned in , the closer the semantic distance icons are to what they represent, the faster participants are. And what is more semantically close than language itself? However, there is debate as to whether semantic distance provides a larger performance boost to older users versus younger. Liu et al.\@ found that further semantic distance disproportionately hindered the reaction times of older participants compared to younger participants \cite{liu_study_2023}; Dosso and Chevalier also found similar results \cite{dosso_how_2021}. But these age-based discrepancies are contested by papers written by Ganor and Te'eni \cite{ganor_designing_nodate} and Schröder and Ziefle \cite{schroder_effects_2008}, so this adds a new perspective into this inconclusive, longstanding HCI debate.

    Of the literature written on icon accessibility, very few included a Text-Only condition. To some extent, the reason is obvious-- in studying iconography, the goal is to condense large amounts of word-based meaning into a visual iota, usually for UI/UX design purposes. But the general results of Misclick Rate suggest that exploration into a middle ground between alienating language entirely and alienating design principles completely. 

    Of course, these results do not come without room for questioning. One of the main conjectures that will be presented to contextualize the negative impact of IWTL below is that the UI experienced high levels of clutter, to use Grahame et al.\@'s terminology \cite{grahame_age_2004}. Although these results are safely significant, it could be possible that they benefited from lack of clutter, although this would not account for the significant difference between TO and IO specifically. And another possibility is that \enquote{digital native} 18-35 year olds might not have been used to associating text with buttons, especially without the explicit hyperlink formatting. Without this prior condition, it might be the case that this TO boost is no longer significant. But with significant results in Misclick Rate that extends equally to both groups, this is unlikely. 

\hypertarget{4.1.2}{\subsection{Icon with Text Labels Interpretations}}
    The significant results related to the Icon with Text Labels condition were found within models run with Misclick Rate as the dependent variable. In the model of best fit, model 3, IWTL had a coefficient of 6.225 and a p-value of 0.019. Thus, IWTL made accuracy worse (or Misclick Rate higher, specifically by 6.225\%) across the board for both Age Groups, as compared to IO and TO. For transparency, these results were not significant in model 4, for either IWTL by itself or the 60+/IWTL interaction term; IWTL was also not significantly worse in Speed for any model.

    These results directly conflict with the consensus within the literature. Generally, IWTL conditions reap faster and more accurate results as compared to IO conditions. Dosso and Chevalier observed that labeled icons gave older participants speed boosts on par with their younger counterparts \cite{dosso_how_2021}. This finding is seconded by Wu et al.\@ \cite{wu_how_2022}. Wu et al.\@ specifically found that skeuomorphic icons with text outperformed its flat icon and unlabeled counterparts. Song et al.\@'s work finds a more dramatic finding, namely that icons presented with text labels enable older users to perform at the same accuracy levels as their younger counterparts \cite{song_developing_2024}. There is some contention from Islam, whose combined quantitative and behavioral study concluded there was no frontrunner between IO and IWTL conditions \cite{islam_exploring_2015}, but the sect within HCI which disagrees with IWTL's positive performance benefits is small.

    So why does IWTL significantly and negatively impact accuracy in this experiment? To propose an explanation, these studies were all conducted in a laboratory setting where icons (or icons with text labels) were shown in isolation. This is not the case in a task-based setting such as this thesis. Icons now exist within a fuller context, which brings along confounding variables such as clutter \cite{grahame_age_2004} that do not exist within a controlled, sterile environment. This is both a combination of a change in environment as well as experimental design error, which especially shone through when combined with already complex UIs such as Website 3.

\hypertarget{4.2}{\section{Limitations}}
    Now, we move towards the limitations of this study, some of which were touched upon by \hyperlink{4.1}{4.1} already.

    The first order of business is acknowledging faulty experimental design. This existed in terms of UI/UX design choices, which was made apparent by the outstanding slowness and error rates on Website 3, and also in terms of general instructions provided. Although much of the error which arose with Website 1 could be attributed with user error, some of it could have originated in unclear instructions. The instruction screen looked something like this:

    \end{figure}

    This was initially altered to provide more clarity (e.g. with wording such as \enquote{Task 1: Switch to image search}), but the original version was shown to participants due to a Maze bug. 

    Another limitation related to experimental design was the decision to host the experiment online and asynchronously through Prolific. There are justifications for this choice (see ) and precedent within both HCI and larger fields that feed into HCI, but this could be pointed towards as the main source for the high drop-off rate of roughly 38/9\%. 

    One of the main questions that arose after seeing the skewed proportion of dropped-off older versus younger users (see , e.g. for IO, 6 18-35 users did not finish and 17 60+ users did not finish) was whether \enquote{tech-savvy} older users were being disproportionately represented in the dataset, as compared to \enquote{non-tech-savvy} older users. Sampling from a pool of already technologically-fluent users was another concern about sampling from Prolific (see ), and perhaps the choice to use numbers from only users who fully completed the study exacerbated this.

    The reasoning for not including them within the dataset was that they would have contributed to the already high levels of noise within the dataset. That is to be expected, but when compounded against the noise that was derived from general user error in relation to the online and asynchronous nature of this study, it becomes concerning. A trend that was qualitatively observed was that users who dropped-off after making it to the UIs would \enquote{give up} after realizing they didn't get the answer immediately. This would result in random clicking, erratic mouse movements, etc., which would overinflate slowness and error within the findings. As mentioned by Mahmud et al.\@, older users specifically exhibit a larger amount of unproductive behaviors stemming from frustration due to obstruction from task-completion as compared to younger Age Groups \cite{mahmud_learning_2020}, which might explain the higher drop-off rate for older users as compared to younger.

    In general, much of this could have been mitigated by conducting an in-person experiment. Any percentage of the drop-off that was due to technical error would be eradicated, first and foremost. But with user error, in-person experiments can perform \enquote{quality control} on participants, which ensure their willingness to give a proper attempt, which despite the monetary reward, is not always the norm on online recruitment platforms such as Prolific. User error also could have been subdued with guidance from experiment leads when confusion arose during the tasks as well.

    The last limitation that will be acknowledged is tracking accuracy via Misclick Rate. This is due to its rapid growth rate, which creates poles on both ends and skews reporting statistics such as means and medians. This does not negate its usefulness entirely, especially as a metric which provides insight into an additional dimension of task-completion (accuracy), but it certainly made Results based on these aforementioned statistics less interpretable. It should be mentioned that this statistic is not without backing in the Background Literature \cite{yu_reducing_2024}, but accuracy in general is not reported on as much in part because of an inability to find a more suitable alternative. For instance, converting Misclick Rate into its numerator, the number of incorrect clicks, would still result in two tails, especially with the added phenomenon of random clicking that was prevalent through this online study.

\hypertarget{4.3}{\section{Extensions}}
    One of the main questions lingering still is whether or not this exciting interaction between TO and 60+ is context-specific. The Websites provided in this study were selected mainly for their common functions in daily life, but without specific criterion for UI design. A future direction that could be pursued is replicating this study with a different set of UIs, or even a more curated sequence of interfaces. 

    Another main extension would be to replicate this study in-person. Achieving the same sample size would be difficult, but would be a worthy endeavor, especially given the limitations of online studies outlined above. This would also fall more in line with the standards for current HCI experimental design, but these will shift as fields that have adopted online studies more completely into their toolkit continue to influence the world of HCI.

    Some other directions that would be less important, but still interesting, to explore would be rotating around the order of appearance of the current UIs (this, alongside altering the instruction screens, could remove the outlier nature of Website 1), and changing what type of icons are displayed (currently, icons more akin to flat icons discussed in are used, but it could be worth exploring skeuomorphic iconography or alternative forms of iconography instead.

\hypertarget{4.4}{\section{Conclusion}}

    As technology continues to evolve in rapid, perhaps even unsustainable, rates, the needs of users are required to be centered more than ever before. User-centric design is a real concept and is touted as the golden standard of product development in the technological sphere, but is not all-inclusive. Instead, software development and UI/UX design center the conventional user, someone with fluency in technological navigation and no lack in cognitive, visual, motor, etc. ability. This myth of the everyday user sidelines many people of varying abilities, including user populations such as the elderly who experience unique combination of disabilities associated with aging. 

    Age-based accessibility research in Human-Computer Interactions aims to unearth various ways of making the technological experiences of older users easier and more productive. These findings require flexibility, as age-based declines do not have a definitive prototype. 

    Icon Type is one of the research fields within this discipline which has been hotly contested. Results on whether certain alterations benefit users, such as skeuomorphic versus flat icon design and with or without text labels, are all in flux. It is also a field that has primarily existed within the context of laboratory experimentation. But with the need for more conclusions, especially conclusions that are relevant to the everyday use cases of these icons, there became a gap that this thesis could fill.

    This thesis explored whether different Icon Types, namely Icon-Only, Text-Only, and Icon with Text Labels, when presented in the context of a web-based task, had different age-based performance effects. Each participant was assigned a Icon Type condition and solved the same sequence of 6 UIs online and asynchronously. 
    
    A total of 240 users completed this experiment, with roughly ~80 users per Icon Type and ~40 users per Icon Type and Age Group subdivisions. After excluding Websites 1 and 3, the main finding from this study was that the Text-Only condition disproportionately positively impacted the Speed of the 60+ Age Group. Alongside that, Text-Only was proven to be significantly positive for accuracy, and Icon with Text Labels was proven to be significantly negative for accuracy.
